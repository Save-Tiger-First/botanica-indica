## 1. Executive perspective

Save Tiger First has an opportunity to develop a technology-enabled conservation ecosystem that uses scientific knowledge, responsible technology and collaboration with people and institutions to support the protection and restoration of nature.

The strategic proposition is not that technology can replace ecological knowledge, conservation practice or local stewardship. It is that well-governed technology can help people observe nature, understand change, make better decisions, coordinate interventions and evaluate outcomes.

The central principle is:

> Technology should work with nature and the people who care for it. It should strengthen ecological understanding and conservation action, not become an end in itself.

This document is informed in part by the TEDxVitosha talk featuring Mirella Goodrum and the NatureTech framing supplied for STF discussion. The talk is treated as a strategic prompt, not as scientific evidence or an independently verified assessment of any specific technology.

## 2. Strategic intent

### 2.1 Proposed vision

A world in which accessible scientific knowledge, responsible technology and collaborative action help people understand, protect and restore biodiversity.

### 2.2 Proposed mission

Build and support reliable digital and data capabilities that make biodiversity knowledge more accessible, improve the quality of conservation evidence, and help communities, researchers and conservation practitioners make informed decisions.

### 2.3 Strategic positioning

STF should aim to become a **Nature Intelligence and Conservation Technology organization**: an ecosystem builder that combines biodiversity data, scientific methods, digital tools and conservation partnerships.

This is a proposed positioning statement for discussion. It should not be presented as an established organizational mandate until STF leadership approves it.

## 3. What NatureTech means for STF

The Nature Tech Collective describes NatureTech broadly: solutions that enable, accelerate or scale a transition to a nature-positive economy. Its framing extends beyond digital products and hardware to processes, institutions and organizational innovations.

For STF, NatureTech should therefore be understood as a means to support nature-positive outcomes, not as a product category or a synonym for artificial intelligence.

Potential technology and capability classes include:

- biodiversity information systems and scientific data infrastructure;
- AI-assisted identification and classification;
- field data collection and mobile tools;
- remote sensing, satellite imagery and geospatial analysis;
- acoustic monitoring and environmental sensors;
- environmental DNA and laboratory-linked evidence;
- restoration planning and monitoring;
- decision-support, reporting and verification;
- community participation, knowledge stewardship and accessible education.

STF should not attempt to build all of these capabilities at once. Each proposed initiative must begin with a clearly evidenced conservation or biodiversity knowledge need.

## 4. Nature as a partner: operating principles

The nature-as-partner idea can be translated into the following design principles.

1. **Ecological integrity before technological novelty.** Select tools because they address a real ecological or societal need.
2. **Complement nature's processes.** Prefer approaches that protect ecosystem functions, resilience and connectivity rather than treating ecosystems as passive infrastructure.
3. **Respect context and scale.** Ecological systems operate across sites, landscapes, watersheds and regions. A site-level intervention must be evaluated in its wider context.
4. **Respect people and rights-holders.** Local communities, Indigenous Peoples, landholders and other affected stakeholders must not be treated merely as data sources.
5. **Evidence before claims.** Distinguish activities, outputs, outcomes and long-term impacts.
6. **Uncertainty is information.** Report confidence, evidence gaps, limitations and conflicting findings.
7. **Adaptive management.** Monitor, learn, disclose failures and change interventions when evidence requires it.
8. **Long-term stewardship.** Consider maintenance, governance, financing, permanence and post-project monitoring.
9. **Responsible data governance.** Protect sensitive species locations, personal information, traditional knowledge and third-party intellectual property.
10. **Interoperability and openness with safeguards.** Use standards and share data where rights, privacy, conservation sensitivity and source terms permit.

## 5. Proposed STF ecosystem structure

STF should distinguish reusable platform capabilities from domain-specific applications.

### 5.1 Shared platform capabilities

Potential shared capabilities include:

- identity, authentication, authorization and organization membership;
- media and document management;
- geographic services and spatial access controls;
- data provenance, source registry and rights management;
- API and integration infrastructure;
- search and discovery;
- notifications and workflow;
- audit, security, observability and analytics;
- data export, interoperability and controlled access;
- common AI/ML operations where appropriate.

A capability should be shared only when there is a real cross-project need, a clear owner, a support model and measurable reuse value. Shared infrastructure must not become a central bottleneck or a route to unrestricted cross-domain data access.

### 5.2 Domain applications

Potential future domains are illustrative and subject to feasibility, partnerships and strategic approval.

**Botanica Indica — botanical intelligence**
- Indian plant identification and exploration;
- taxonomy, nomenclature and scientific references;
- plant observations and occurrence evidence;
- geographic and habitat context;
- expert review and community contribution.

**Wildlife and fauna intelligence — future possibility**
- species information and evidence-based identification;

- responsible handling of sensitive species locations;
- links to authorized monitoring programmes.

**Ecosystem and habitat intelligence — future possibility**
- habitat characterization and change detection;
- landscape and ecological connectivity analysis;
- field and remote-sensing evidence;

**Conservation intervention and impact monitoring — future possibility**
- intervention planning and baseline documentation;
- ecological and social monitoring;

These are not approved product commitments. Botanica Indica remains the first domain application and the immediate delivery priority.

## 6. Conservation impact logic

Every STF initiative should state how its technology is expected to contribute to a conservation or biodiversity outcome.

Use a transparent results chain:

1. **Need or challenge:** What ecological or societal problem is being addressed?
2. **Baseline:** What is known about the initial condition, and how was it measured?
3. **Inputs:** What resources, partners, data and capabilities are required?
4. **Activities:** What will the project actually do?
5. **Outputs:** What immediate products or services will be delivered?
6. **Outcomes:** What changes in knowledge, behaviour, management or ecological condition are expected?
7. **Impact:** What longer-term contribution to biodiversity, ecosystem integrity or human well-being is supported by evidence?
8. **Assumptions and risks:** What must be true for the results chain to hold?
9. **Monitoring and learning:** How will results, unintended effects and uncertainty be evaluated?

Do not claim that an application, sensor, model, dashboard or dataset has conserved biodiversity merely because it has been deployed or used.

### 6.1 Example: Botanica Indica

| Results level | Illustrative measure |
|---|---|
| Activity | Guided plant observations and identification workflows |
| Output | Records with images, locality precision, provenance and review status |
| Intermediate outcome | Improved access to reliable botanical information and better-documented observations |
| Potential conservation contribution | Better evidence for botanical research, habitat understanding and informed decisions |
| Impact claim | Requires independent ecological evidence and an appropriate causal evaluation |

Botanica Indica should not claim direct improvement in ecosystem condition unless a linked intervention and monitoring programme establish that outcome.

## 7. Nature-based Solutions as a reference framework

The IUCN Global Standard for Nature-based Solutions (NbS) is a useful reference when STF evaluates future ecosystem protection, management or restoration interventions.

The current IUCN/INBS presentation describes eight criteria and 27 indicators. The criteria address:

1. societal challenges;
2. integrated systems perspective;
3. enhanced biodiversity, ecosystem integrity and connectivity;
4. financial feasibility and economic justification;
5. inclusive governance and equity;
6. balancing goals and impacts;
7. adaptive management;
8. sustainability and mainstreaming.

The IUCN standard is a framework for designing, assessing and improving Nature-based Solutions. It is not a generic badge for any conservation technology project. STF must not claim compliance, verification or certification without following the applicable standard and assessment process.

Use the standard proportionately:
- as a design reference when an intervention is genuinely a Nature-based Solution;
- as a checklist for risks, trade-offs, governance and long-term sustainability;
- as a monitoring and learning framework;
- not as a substitute for ecological baselines, field evidence or independent review.

## 8. Technology selection framework

Every proposed technology should be assessed against the problem and the alternatives.

| Evaluation dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| Ecological relevance | Does it address a defined conservation or biodiversity need? |
| Evidence | Is there credible evidence that the method works in the intended context? |
| Additionality | Does it add value beyond existing scientific or conservation practice? |
| Biodiversity safeguards | Could deployment disturb wildlife, habitats or ecological processes? |
| Social legitimacy | Have affected communities and rights-holders been involved appropriately? |
| Data quality | Are uncertainty, bias, sampling effort and validation documented? |
| Interoperability | Can results be used with established standards and partner systems? |
| Rights and governance | Are data, images, traditional knowledge and location permissions clear? |
| Lifecycle cost | Can the technology be operated, maintained and updated sustainably? |
| Alternatives | Would a simpler, lower-cost or non-digital intervention work better? |
| Evaluation | Can outputs and outcomes be measured credibly? |
| Exit and portability | Can data and workflows survive a vendor, model or platform change? |

A technology should not be selected merely because it is innovative, attracts funding or produces visually compelling demonstrations.

## 9. Data, science and AI governance

STF should apply a common governance baseline across domain applications.

### 9.1 Provenance and reproducibility
- Record data source, contributor, date, method, version and transformation history.
- Preserve source statements separately from normalized interpretations.
- Record model, software, configuration and evaluation versions.
- Make scientific assertions attributable and reviewable.

### 9.2 Uncertainty and validation
- Separate model score, calibrated probability, evidence quality and expert confidence.
- Preserve alternative hypotheses and disagreement.
- Allow insufficient evidence and unknown outcomes.
- Never convert an AI candidate into a verified scientific record without the required validation.

### 9.3 Rights and privacy
- Evaluate display, redistribution, commercial use, derivative use and AI training separately.
- Do not infer AI-training permission from public visibility or download access.
- Protect precise locations for threatened, exploited or otherwise sensitive taxa.
- Respect contributor consent, community protocols and restrictions on traditional knowledge.
- Define retention, deletion, withdrawal and access policies.

### 9.4 Standards and interoperability
Use established standards where they fit the domain, including Darwin Core and Taxon Concept Standard for biodiversity data. Preserve domain semantics that exchange standards do not fully represent. Publish explicit, versioned mappings rather than treating an export format as the internal system of record.

## 10. India-first implementation context

For Indian biodiversity initiatives, STF should plan for:

- India's varied biogeographic regions, ecosystems and administrative geography;
- state, district and local ecological context;
- native, endemic, introduced, naturalized, cultivated and invasive status distinctions;
- multilingual and regional-language accessibility;
- institutional botanical and zoological expertise;
- herbaria, universities, research institutions and conservation organizations;
- local communities, forest-dependent people and relevant rights-holders;
- data protection, wildlife safeguards and applicable Indian legal requirements;
- variable connectivity and field conditions.

Do not assume that a dataset is authoritative for every taxonomic group, state or use case. Authority and fitness for purpose must be documented at source and taxonomic-scope level.

## 11. Partnership model

STF should not attempt to become the sole producer of all ecological knowledge.

Potential partner categories include:
- botanical and zoological institutions;
- universities and research laboratories;
- herbaria, museums and biodiversity repositories;
- conservation NGOs and protected-area practitioners;
- community organizations and local knowledge holders;
- geospatial and remote-sensing research groups;
- technology providers and responsible NatureTech enterprises;
- government agencies and public data programmes.

Partnerships should define scientific responsibility, data ownership and licensing, publication rights, attribution, privacy, access to sensitive information, review responsibilities and exit arrangements.

## 12. Prioritization and staged roadmap

### Stage 1 — Establish the foundation
- Complete Botanica Indica's scientific and data architecture review.
- Establish shared principles for provenance, rights, privacy and scientific validation.
- Deliver a reliable first domain application.
- Develop a small number of expert and institutional relationships.
- Define outcome measures for the product itself without overstating conservation impact.

### Stage 2 — Demonstrate real-world value
- Test the product with botanists, students, researchers and field contributors.
- Evaluate identification performance across regions, taxa, image conditions and user groups.
- Measure record quality, expert workload, source coverage and user comprehension of uncertainty.
- Publish transparent limitations and evaluation results.

### Stage 3 — Reuse platform capabilities
- Identify genuinely reusable services from operational evidence.
- Separate shared platform capabilities from botanical domain rules.
- Establish service ownership, support, security and cost allocation.
- Avoid premature microservices or a broad platform build without consumers.

### Stage 4 — Pilot a second domain
- Select one conservation problem with a committed scientific or implementation partner.
- Validate the need, baseline, rights, safeguards and outcomes before product design.
- Reuse shared services only where they fit.
- Evaluate the pilot before creating a permanent application.

### Stage 5 — Expand the ecosystem selectively
- Add domains only where STF has credible expertise, partners, governance and a sustainable operating model.
- Build cross-domain intelligence where shared evidence adds genuine value.
- Report conservation outcomes with appropriate attribution and independent evaluation.

## 13. Suggested strategic performance indicators

Use a balanced scorecard. Do not combine all measures into one impact score.

**Scientific quality**
- proportion of records with traceable provenance;
- proportion with adequate diagnostic evidence;
- expert review agreement and disagreement rates;
- taxonomy reconciliation coverage;
- source and geographic coverage gaps.

**Product and platform quality**
- task completion and user comprehension;
- identification performance by taxonomic and geographic segment;
- availability, response time and operational cost;
- accessibility and language coverage;
- data portability and standards conformance.

**Contribution and partnership**
- active expert contributors;
- institutional and community partnerships with documented agreements;
- review turnaround and unresolved backlog;
- contributor retention and feedback.

**Conservation relevance**
- decisions or research activities supported by documented evidence;
- monitoring programmes using validated STF outputs;
- measured change in defined ecological indicators where a project has a valid baseline and monitoring design;
- safeguards, grievances and unintended impacts reported and addressed.

Metrics should be disaggregated by geography, taxonomic group, evidence type and relevant user group. Report denominators and sampling limitations.

## 14. Risks and anti-patterns

STF should actively guard against:

- technology-first projects without a defined ecological need;
- confusing AI predictions with scientific validation;
- equating more observations with better biodiversity outcomes;
- equating tree-planting counts with ecosystem restoration;
- using remote-sensing classifications without field validation;
- publishing sensitive species coordinates;
- extracting local or Indigenous knowledge without consent and governance;
- treating open access as unrestricted reuse or AI-training permission;
- overstating carbon, biodiversity or restoration impact;
- building shared infrastructure without real consumers or service ownership;
- expanding into multiple domains before the first application is reliable;
- relying on one vendor, model, dataset or cloud provider without exit planning.

- the partnership and community engagement responsibilities;
- the funding and long-term operations model.

Every conservation-impact claim should identify its evidence, method, time period, limitations and responsible reviewer.

## 19. Human–Nature Connectedness: the human dimension of conservation

### 19.1 Strategic context

The TEDxGöteborg talk, “Nature Isn’t a Place – It’s Who You Are”, featuring Laurence Nachin, introduces a complementary perspective: humans are not separate from nature, and nature should not be treated only as a destination outside everyday life. The TEDx Talks video description itself summarizes this framing and specifically connects her microbiology and cell biology background with nature facilitation, organizational practice, well-being, creativity and pro-environmental behaviour. The talk was published on 17 December 2024.

Nachin's official Sense in Nature profile confirms her background as a microbiologist with a PhD in microbiology and cellular biology, her experience in research and R&D, her training as a nature and forest therapy guide, and her founding of Sense in Nature in 2019. Her stated work includes integrating nature into organizational thinking and decision-making.

Her official profile also describes how her scientific experience at cellular and molecular scales informed her understanding of interdependence, and how she seeks to bring nature connection into workplaces and everyday organizational practice.

This is a strategic and educational perspective. It should not be treated as a substitute for ecological science, clinical evidence or formal evaluation.

### 19.2 Proposed STF principle

**People are part of nature, not visitors to it.**

STF should communicate conservation not only as the protection of distant forests, wildlife reserves and charismatic species, but also as recognition of the ecological relationships that sustain everyday human life.

This principle can inform:
- public biodiversity education;
- urban and peri-urban nature engagement;
- schools, colleges and workplace programmes;
- citizen-science and field observation;
- responsible outdoor experiences;
- sustainable organizational practices;
- conservation communication that connects local actions to wider ecosystems.

It does not mean that every human activity is ecologically beneficial, or that human well-being and ecological integrity always align without trade-offs.

### 19.3 Evidence and interpretation

Research supports a meaningful relationship between human–nature connectedness and several environmental and well-being outcomes, but the strength and causal interpretation of the evidence vary.

A 2020 meta-analysis by Whitburn, Linklater and Abrahamse examined 37 samples involving 13,237 participants. It found a positive association between connection to nature and pro-environmental behaviour (reported pooled correlation r = 0.42, 95% CI 0.36–0.47). The authors also reported substantial heterogeneity among studies and noted the need for more longitudinal and randomized research to establish causation.

A 2022 global meta-analysis by Richardson and colleagues examined 147 correlational studies and 59 experimental studies. It found that greater human–nature connectedness was associated with more pro-nature behaviours and better health, and that some interventions involving nature contact and mindfulness increased connectedness. These findings support connectedness as a potentially useful sustainability pathway; they do not establish that every intervention produces durable behaviour change.

A 2025 systematic review of 29 studies reported a positive relationship between connectedness to nature and pro-environmental behaviours, while also noting variation across studies and differences between individual behaviours and activism-related behaviours.

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of nature-based activities reported increases in human–nature connectedness across the six activity types examined. The size of change varied by activity, with mindfulness and wildlife encounters showing larger changes than some other activity types in that analysis. This does not establish that one activity is universally best across cultures, ages, accessibility needs or ecological contexts.

A 2026 review of conceptual issues in nature connection and well-being emphasizes that the field contains conceptual tensions: the idea that humans are part of nature is sometimes studied using methods that implicitly treat humans and the environment as separate. This is an important caution for STF's language and evaluation design.

### 19.4 Implications for STF programmes

STF may consider human–nature connectedness as a cross-cutting educational and engagement outcome, not as a substitute for conservation impact.

Possible programme formats include:
- guided biodiversity observation and nature journaling;
- neighbourhood and campus biodiversity walks;
- workplace biodiversity learning and reflection;
- school-based ecological literacy activities;
- participatory mapping of everyday biodiversity;
- field activities linking species observations to habitat, seasonality and ecological relationships;
- workshops that connect personal choices, organizational practices and ecosystem pressures.

Programmes should be co-designed with appropriate educators, ecologists, facilitators and community partners. Nature and forest therapy activities should be led by appropriately trained practitioners and should not be marketed as clinical treatment unless delivered under the relevant professional and regulatory framework.

### 19.5 Evaluation: do not collapse connection, behaviour and ecological outcome

If STF pilots a human–nature connectedness programme, evaluate at least four distinct dimensions:

1. **Experience and participation:** who participated, accessibility, duration, setting, facilitation and participant safety.
2. **Connectedness and learning:** changes in participants' self-reported relationship with nature, ecological knowledge and ability to recognize local biodiversity.
3. **Behaviour:** observed or credibly documented changes in relevant practices, participation, stewardship or organizational decisions.
4. **Ecological outcomes:** changes in defined ecological indicators measured with suitable baselines, methods, timeframes and independent review where appropriate.

Use validated measures where possible, and report the instrument, language adaptation, sampling method, baseline, follow-up period, missing data and limitations. Self-reported intention should not be presented as verified behaviour. Increased participation should not be presented as biodiversity recovery.

### 19.6 Workplace and organizational implications

Nachin's Sense in Nature work explicitly considers how nature connection can be integrated into organizational thinking and workplace processes. This offers STF a potential future application area: helping organizations connect biodiversity literacy with decisions about procurement, campuses, facilities, travel, landscaping, resource use and community engagement.

A workplace programme should not be reduced to indoor plants, motivational messaging or a one-off outdoor event. It should connect learning to actual organizational decisions and measure whether practices change.

### 19.7 Safeguards and inclusion

- Do not imply that people who cannot access green spaces are less responsible or less connected to nature.
- Include accessible, culturally appropriate and locally relevant ways to engage with nature.
- Respect local communities, customary practices and knowledge rights.
- Avoid disturbing wildlife or damaging habitats during facilitated activities.
- Apply local access rules, safety planning, weather assessment and a leave-no-trace approach.
- Avoid unsubstantiated therapeutic or health claims.
- Recognize that nature connection is culturally and individually variable; do not prescribe a single emotional response to nature.

### 19.8 Strategic synthesis

The two talks supplied for STF discussion can be understood as complementary:

- Mirella Goodrum's NatureTech framing: work with nature and use technology to support ecological understanding and action.
- Laurence Nachin's human–nature connectedness framing: recognize that people are already part of natural systems and integrate this understanding into everyday life and decisions.

Together they suggest a broader STF approach: **connect people with nature, build reliable knowledge about nature, support decisions that respect ecological systems, and evaluate whether those decisions produce meaningful outcomes.**

This synthesis is a proposed strategic interpretation by STF, not a quotation or formal position attributed to either speaker.

## 20. Forest Networks, Mycorrhizal Ecology and Biodiversity Resilience

### 20.1 Talk and strategic context

The official TEDxSeattle listing gives the title as “Nature’s internet: how trees talk to each other in a healthy forest” and identifies Simard as a Professor of Forest Ecology in the Department of Forest and Conservation Sciences at the University of British Columbia. The supplied YouTube video is the corresponding TEDxSeattle talk (published in 2017).

The talk develops several connected ideas:
- mycorrhizal fungi form symbiotic relationships with plant roots and can connect multiple plants through fungal mycelia;
- DNA-based methods can help identify fungal genets and patterns of below-ground connectivity;
- isotope-tracing experiments have investigated movement of carbon and other elements between plants;
- Simard's “hub tree” or “mother tree” framing emphasizes the ecological importance of large, old trees and their relationships with surrounding organisms;
- forest composition and interactions among plants, fungi, animals, soils, water and people influence ecosystem processes;
- the Coast Salish concept of reciprocity is presented as an ethical and relational perspective on stewardship.

The talk is valuable as a way to communicate forest complexity and interdependence. It is not, by itself, a current consensus statement for every mechanism it describes. In particular, the talk's metaphorical language should not be repeated as if it were a settled mechanistic description.

### 20.2 What is well established

**Mycorrhizae are important ecological symbioses.** Many plants form associations with mycorrhizal fungi. Fungal hyphae can extend beyond roots and improve access to nutrients and water; plants supply photosynthetically derived carbon to their fungal partners. The type and outcome of the relationship vary with plant species, fungal identity, soil conditions and environmental context.

**Common mycorrhizal networks are biologically possible.** Fungal individuals or mycelial systems can connect roots of multiple plants. Research has investigated the extent, persistence and ecological function of these connections. Their prevalence and functional importance vary by ecosystem, fungal guild, host compatibility and experimental method.

**Stable-isotope methods can detect movement of elements.** Isotopic labelling is a powerful method for tracing carbon or nutrients through ecosystems. Detection of a small isotopic signal, however, does not by itself establish the pathway, direction, magnitude, net benefit or ecological significance of transfer.

**Forest ecosystems are interaction networks.** Trees, shrubs, herbs, fungi, microbes, animals, soils, hydrology, disturbance and climate interact. Forest structure and composition can influence regeneration, nutrient cycling, habitat, productivity and resilience. These processes are context-dependent and cannot be reduced to one underground network.

### 20.3 Important scientific qualification: the “mother tree” hypothesis

The popular account of mature “mother trees” preferentially transferring substantial carbon to their offspring through common mycorrhizal networks is contested.

A 2023 review by Henriksson and colleagues, “Re-examining the evidence for the mother tree hypothesis”, concluded that evidence for significant net carbon transfer through common mycorrhizal networks that benefits recipient trees was still lacking. The authors discuss methodological challenges in interpreting small isotope shifts and note that evidence of movement is not equivalent to evidence of a meaningful growth benefit.

A 2023 perspective by Karst, Jones and Hoeksema, “Positive citation bias and overinterpreted results lead to misinformation on common mycorrhizal networks in forests”, argued that three frequently repeated claims were insufficiently supported: that such networks are widespread in forests, that network-mediated resource transfer generally improves seedling performance, and that mature trees preferentially send resources or defence signals to offspring. They concluded that the evidence was then too sparse and unsettled to inform forest management.

A 2024 article in *Trends in Plant Science*, “Mother trees, altruistic fungi, and the perils of plant personification”, further cautioned against attributing human-like motives or altruism to plants and fungi.

The literature is not a simple binary. A 2024 perspective by Merckx and colleagues argues that mycoheterotrophic plants provide important natural evidence for resource transfer mediated by shared mycorrhizal fungi in particular ecological contexts. This is a substantive counterpoint to the 2023 critiques and should be represented fairly. It does not, however, establish the broad popular claim that mature trees routinely nourish their offspring or communicate warnings through a forest-wide network. A 2024–2025 systematic mapping of experimental approaches in arbuscular mycorrhiza also found substantial methodological and field-evidence gaps, underscoring that the debate concerns both the existence of fungal connections and what particular experimental designs can demonstrate about their effects.

**STF communication rule:** distinguish (a) fungal symbiosis and root associations, (b) evidence of physical connectivity, (c) observed movement of labelled substances, (d) demonstrated net transfer through a particular pathway, and (e) a measured benefit to the recipient. Do not present these as interchangeable levels of evidence.

### 20.4 “Communication” and warning signals

Plants respond to environmental stress through chemical, physiological and ecological mechanisms. Fungi and other soil organisms can participate in complex plant–microbe interactions. However, claims that trees routinely send intentional warning messages to neighbouring trees through a common mycorrhizal network, or that the network functions as a forest immune system, should not be presented as established general facts.

Where STF communicates a specific signalling result, it should identify:
- the plant and fungal species studied;
- the ecosystem and experimental setting;
- the signal or molecule measured;
- the demonstrated pathway;
- the control conditions and alternative explanations;
- whether a recipient response was measured;
- whether the finding has been independently replicated.

Avoid anthropomorphic language such as “trees talk”, “mother trees feed their children” or “fungi act altruistically” unless clearly labelled as metaphor or as the speaker's framing.

### 20.5 Biodiversity, mixtures and forest resilience

The broader ecological lesson of Simard's talk remains strategically important even where particular network mechanisms are disputed: forests should not be treated as interchangeable collections of timber stems.

Species composition, structural complexity, age distribution, regeneration, soil communities, deadwood, hydrology, disturbance history and landscape connectivity all matter. Diverse forests may provide a wider range of ecological functions and can sometimes be more resistant or resilient to particular disturbances. The relationship is not universal: outcomes depend on species traits, site conditions, management objectives, disturbance regimes and the timescale being considered.

STF should therefore avoid simplistic claims that every mixed forest is always more resilient than every monoculture, or that planting more species automatically restores an ecosystem. The appropriate intervention depends on the reference ecosystem, native species composition, landscape history, threats and local ecological evidence.

### 20.6 Indigenous knowledge, Coast Salish perspectives and ethical use

The talk references Coast Salish teachings and the principle of reciprocity. STF may acknowledge that Indigenous and local knowledge systems contain long-standing understandings of relationships among people, forests, rivers, fish, wildlife and place.

However:
- Coast Salish peoples are diverse Indigenous nations and communities; do not treat them as a single undifferentiated source of knowledge.
- Do not generalize a specific teaching to all Indigenous peoples.
- Do not extract, reproduce or commercialize Indigenous knowledge without appropriate consent, attribution, governance and benefit-sharing.
- Distinguish a speaker's interpretation from knowledge shared by a particular nation or knowledge holder.
- For Indian programmes, engage the relevant Adivasi, Indigenous, forest-dependent and local communities on their own terms and respect their rights, protocols, languages and knowledge governance.
- Scientific validation and respect for knowledge rights are both necessary; neither should be used to erase the other.

### 20.7 Implications for Botanica Indica

This talk strengthens the case for treating plant identification as an entry point into ecological relationships, rather than a stand-alone name lookup.

Potential future botanical profile and learning features could explain:
- whether a plant is known to form arbuscular, ectomycorrhizal or other root-associated fungal relationships, where reliable species-level evidence exists;
- the difference between a plant–fungus symbiosis and a demonstrated common mycorrhizal network;
- ecological roles, habitat associations and known interactions with pollinators, herbivores, dispersers or host organisms;
- what is known, what is inferred and what remains uncertain;
- the geographic and ecological context of the source evidence.

Do not infer a species' mycorrhizal type, network connectivity or ecological role merely from its genus, family or the presence of fungi in a photograph. Such information needs taxon-specific or appropriately qualified evidence.

Botanica Indica should model ecological interactions as evidence-backed, typed relationships with source, context, confidence/uncertainty and temporal applicability. A future interaction knowledge layer may include plant–fungus, plant–pollinator, plant–herbivore, host–parasite, dispersal, competition and facilitation relationships. This is a future capability, not a reason to expand the initial identification scope.

### 20.8 Implications for STF conservation and restoration programmes

For any future forest, restoration or landscape programme, STF should prioritize:
- ecosystem-specific reference conditions and native composition;
- protection of existing old-growth and mature habitat where relevant;
- natural regeneration and recruitment;
- soil, hydrological and disturbance processes;
- landscape connectivity and habitat heterogeneity;
- locally appropriate monitoring;
- community participation and rights;
- transparent reporting of uncertainty and unintended effects.

Do not use the mother-tree narrative as the scientific justification for a restoration intervention. The case for protecting mature trees and diverse forest structure can be made using broader, better-established ecological, biodiversity, carbon, hydrological and habitat evidence.

## 21. Hidden biodiversity beneath the forest: Amazon soil microbiomes and land-use change

### 21.1 The talk and its central message

Jorge Rodrigues's TEDxUTA talk, **“The incredible (endangered) biodiversity of the Amazon rainforest”**, was published by TEDx Talks on 15 November 2013. The official video description identifies Rodrigues as a microbiologist whose research uses high-throughput genomic technologies to study microorganisms in tropical forests and other environments, with a focus on the effects of human-caused environmental change on biodiversity and ecosystem function.

Talk supplied for discussion: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=oAl82gFMZis

The talk makes an important strategic point for STF: biodiversity is not limited to organisms that people can readily see, photograph or identify. Soil microbial communities are part of forest biodiversity and contribute to ecological processes that sustain ecosystem functioning.

The user's timestamped summary highlights forest conversion to pasture, fire and land-use change; microbial diversity and homogenization; nutrient cycling; and the possible biotechnological value of microbial diversity. These are useful themes, but individual numerical statements and broad comparisons in a public talk should not be repeated as universal scientific constants without tracing them to the underlying study, sampling method, soil type, location and analytical definition.

### 21.2 Independent research verification

The central claim that Amazon forest conversion can alter and homogenize soil bacterial communities is supported by peer-reviewed research.

- **Rodrigues et al. (2013), “Conversion of the Amazon rainforest to agriculture results in biotic homogenization of soil bacterial communities.”** The study found that local taxonomic and phylogenetic diversity of soil bacteria increased after conversion in the studied locations, while communities became more similar across space. The homogenization was associated with loss of forest bacteria with restricted ranges. This is a crucial nuance: local richness can rise while regional distinctiveness and overall biodiversity value decline. https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1220608110
- **Venturini et al. (2025), “Linking soil microbial genomic features to forest-to-pasture conversion in the Amazon.”** This study compared forest and pasture soils in the Eastern Brazilian Amazon and found differences in microbial genomes and functional traits, including genes associated with biogeochemical cycling and carbohydrate-active enzymes. It also reported that many recovered genomes had low similarity to known species, highlighting how much microbial diversity remains poorly characterized. https://doi.org/10.1128/spectrum.01561-24
- **“Community-Level Metabolic Shifts Following Land Use Change in the Amazon Rainforest Identified by a Supervised Machine Learning Approach” (2025).** Metagenomic analysis found differences between forest and long-established pasture soil communities, including microbial community structure and gene profiles associated with carbohydrate/energy metabolism and carbon and nitrogen cycling. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40269473/
- **Danielson & Mazza Rodrigues (2022), “Impacts of land-use change on soil microbial communities and their function in the Amazon Rainforest.”** This review chapter summarizes evidence that forest-to-pasture conversion changes microbial community composition and activity, with implications for nutrient and greenhouse-gas cycling. https://doi.org/10.1016/bs.agron.2022.04.001
- **Jiao et al. (2024), “Land conversion to agriculture induces taxonomic homogenization of soil microbial communities globally.”** A continental survey and global meta-analysis found taxonomic and functional homogenization of soil bacterial communities following agricultural conversion. This broader evidence supports the relevance of homogenization beyond the Amazon, but does not remove the need for ecosystem- and region-specific assessment. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-024-47348-8

The evidence supports the direction of the talk's message, but does not justify treating every Amazon soil, microbial group or land-use history as identical. Outcomes depend on soil properties, vegetation, climate, land-use duration, management, sampling depth, forest type and the microbial groups and methods being measured.

### 21.3 Important scientific qualifications

**Microbial diversity is not one number.** Species richness, taxonomic diversity, phylogenetic diversity, functional-gene diversity, metabolic potential and community similarity are different measurements. A claim such as “100 million microbes and 10,000 species in one gram” should be treated as an illustrative statement from the talk unless its exact source, sampling context, taxonomic resolution and counting method are verified. It should not become a standard fact or a Botanica Indica data assumption.

**Homogenization is not simply a reduction in local species count.** The 2013 study is especially instructive because local bacterial richness increased in converted sites while communities became more similar across locations and forest-restricted bacteria were lost. Conservation monitoring should therefore consider beta diversity, spatial turnover and loss of specialist or endemic microbial lineages, not only local richness.

**Land-use change and climate change should not be ranked universally.** The talk's emphasis on forest conversion and fire is well grounded as a major regional pressure in the Amazon context. However, the statement that land-use change is categorically a greater threat than climate change should not be generalized across regions, time horizons or ecological outcomes. Interactions among deforestation, fire, fragmentation, drought and warming can compound risks.

**Microbial ecosystem functions require evidence at the appropriate level.** Microbes participate in carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and other biogeochemical processes. A gene's presence indicates functional potential, not necessarily an in-situ process rate. Stronger claims require suitable functional, experimental or field measurements.

**Bioprospecting is a possibility, not a guaranteed conservation return.** Microbial diversity can be valuable for biotechnology and drug discovery, but the presence of a diverse microbial community does not establish that it contains a clinically useful antibiotic or cancer therapy. Discovery, validation, safety, efficacy, intellectual property, benefit-sharing and regulatory approval are separate stages. STF should not promise medical breakthroughs as a direct consequence of conserving a particular habitat.

### 21.4 What this means for Save Tiger First

The talk strengthens an important principle: conservation should protect ecological complexity, including components that are not immediately visible to people.

For STF's strategy, this suggests:

- Include soil health and below-ground biodiversity in appropriate landscape and restoration programme designs.
- Avoid defining biodiversity only through charismatic fauna, flowering plants or visible species counts.
- Where microbial monitoring is relevant, partner with universities and qualified soil-microbiology laboratories rather than attempting to infer microbial status from ordinary photographs or citizen-science observations.
- Design monitoring around clear questions: microbial community composition, functional potential, soil carbon/nitrogen/phosphorus processes, disturbance history or recovery after restoration.
- Use matched sampling designs and repeat measurements where possible; distinguish reference forest, degraded sites, pasture, regenerating forest and other land-use classes.
- Interpret microbial indicators alongside vegetation, soil chemistry, hydrology, land-use history and ecological outcomes.
- Establish data governance for soil samples, genomic sequences, metadata, access and benefit-sharing before collecting or publishing them.

### 21.5 Relevance to Botanica Indica — and the boundary

Botanica Indica's initial purpose remains India-first plant identification and evidence-backed botanical knowledge. It should not expand into a microbial genomics platform as a result of this talk.

There are, however, future knowledge-layer opportunities:

1. Represent well-sourced plant–microbe and plant–fungus interactions where the evidence is taxon- and context-specific.
2. Link botanical profiles to relevant soil/ecosystem context without implying that a plant photograph can reveal the soil microbiome.
3. Distinguish observed interaction, experimentally demonstrated function, inferred ecological association and general background knowledge.
4. Preserve source, geography, sampling method, date, scale, taxonomic resolution and uncertainty for every interaction claim.
5. Provide links to external genomic datasets or research repositories only where their terms and identifiers permit.

Do not infer microbial associations, nitrogen fixation, pathogen status, mycorrhizal partnerships or soil function from a plant's family or genus alone. Do not add microbial identification features to the first product release without a separate validated scientific and product case.

### 21.6 Strategic implications for conservation and restoration

For STF programmes, the strongest practical implication is to move from tree-cover-only thinking toward ecosystem condition and function. Depending on the site and programme objectives, relevant measures may include native vegetation recovery, soil organic matter, erosion, soil compaction, water infiltration, nutrient cycling, microbial community indicators, habitat connectivity and community-defined benefits.

These indicators should be selected with local ecologists and soil scientists. No single microbial metric should be treated as a universal score of ecosystem health, and microbial recovery should not be assumed to follow automatically from tree planting or canopy cover increase.

The Amazon is a valuable case study, not a direct ecological template for India. Any application to Indian forests, grasslands, wetlands, plantations or restoration sites must be based on local ecosystem types, soil conditions, land-use history, indigenous and local knowledge, and India-specific field evidence.

### 21.7 Strategic synthesis across the first four talks

Together, the four talks suggest a broader STF approach:

- **NatureTech:** use technology to support nature rather than treating nature as a passive resource.
- **Human–nature connectedness:** strengthen the understanding that people are part of ecological systems.
- **Forest interdependence:** recognize complex ecological relationships while distinguishing demonstrated mechanisms from popular simplifications.
- **Hidden microbial biodiversity:** include below-ground life and ecosystem processes in the meaning and measurement of biodiversity.

A proposed STF principle follows:

**Make the visible and invisible dimensions of biodiversity understandable; connect people with nature; use technology responsibly; and protect ecological relationships through evidence-based action.**

This is a proposed strategic interpretation, not a scientific consensus statement or an approved conservation intervention.


### 21.10 A second soil-biodiversity perspective: Dr. Emanga Alobwede

Dr. Emanga Alobwede's TEDxWoodLaneWomen talk, **“How tiny organisms shape our planet”**, was published on 31 January 2025: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zuqMcNg8KLU. The official TEDx description identifies her as an interdisciplinary environmental scientist with academic, business and government experience, including work in soil science and environmental biotechnology.

The talk complements the Amazon case in Section 21. It shifts the message from a particular tropical forest conversion study to the wider importance of below-ground biodiversity for food systems, soil function and ecosystem resilience.

**Independent verification:** FAO's *State of Knowledge of Soil Biodiversity* explains that soil biodiversity includes organisms across microbial, microfaunal, mesofaunal, macrofaunal and megafaunal groups. Soil organisms contribute to nutrient transformations, plant productivity, carbon and nitrogen cycling, water filtration, contaminant degradation and medicine. FAO also identifies deforestation, fire, agricultural intensification, pollution and urbanization among pressures on soil biodiversity. Its guidance calls for long-term information, standardized sampling and analysis protocols, and stronger evidence on soil organism groups and their functions.  
Sources: https://www.fao.org/interactive/soil-biodiversity/en/ and https://www.fao.org/soils-portal/soil-biodiversity/en

**Critical qualifications:**

- Soil microbes are not a single functional group. Bacteria, archaea, fungi, protists and other soil organisms differ in ecology, function and response to disturbance.
- Soil carbon outcomes are not automatically positive. Soil organisms participate in carbon and nitrogen transformations that can support carbon storage or greenhouse-gas emissions, depending on environmental conditions and processes.
- Microbial inoculants and biofertilizers are not universal substitutes for mineral fertilizers. Effectiveness depends on crop, soil, climate, strain, formulation, management and field conditions; claims require replicated, context-specific trials.
- The talk's potential climate and agricultural benefits should not be interpreted as evidence that any microbial intervention will reliably sequester carbon or increase yields.
- The talk's claims about particular microbial counts or universal proportions of food derived from soil should not be repeated as fixed constants without tracing the source and its methodology.

**STF recommendation:** Soil biodiversity should be an explicit consideration in appropriate restoration, agriculture and landscape programmes. It should not become a new core Botanica Indica feature or a new STF laboratory programme without qualified partners, a clear ecological question, sampling design, permits, budget, long-term monitoring and data governance.

### 21.11 Strategic synthesis across the soil-biodiversity talks

The Jorge Rodrigues and Emanga Alobwede talks reinforce a common strategic insight: visible species inventories do not capture the full biodiversity or functioning of an ecosystem. STF should adopt an **above-ground and below-ground ecological perspective**, while distinguishing biodiversity description from functional measurement.

A practical future soil-health pilot, if justified, should:
1. Be co-designed with landholders, relevant communities and qualified soil ecologists.
2. Define a limited question and baseline before sampling.
3. Use validated laboratory methods, documented sample handling and metadata.
4. Measure soil physical and chemical context alongside microbial indicators.
5. Use repeated, comparable sampling and report uncertainty.
6. Separate microbial composition, functional potential and measured ecosystem process rates.
7. Publish only data and locations that are authorized and ecologically safe.

**Decision status:** Proposed strategic direction; no soil-microbiome monitoring programme is approved by this document.

## 23. Urban biodiversity, migratory connectivity and the Beijing Swift

### 23.1 The talk and its strategic contribution

Terry Townshend's TEDxShanghai talk, **“The incredible life of the Beijing swift”**, was published by TEDx Talks on 22 April 2025: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=E6h53zVSm8M.

The talk uses the Beijing Swift (*Apus apus pekinensis*), a subspecies of the Common Swift, to show how a city can be both a breeding habitat and a point of connection to ecosystems thousands of kilometres away. The story combines migration research, historic nesting relationships with Beijing's built heritage, tracking technology, public engagement and practical action by students, institutions and building owners.

Its strategic value for STF is broader than bird conservation. It demonstrates that urban biodiversity is not separate from landscape and global biodiversity: cities can provide breeding, roosting, feeding or stopover habitat, and urban decisions can affect migratory species whose annual cycles cross many jurisdictions.

### 23.2 Independent research verification: migration distance and time in flight

The peer-reviewed 2022 study by Zhao et al., **“A 30,000-km journey by *Apus apus pekinensis* tracks arid lands between northern China and south-western Africa”**, used light-level geolocators on adult Beijing-breeding birds and analysed complete annual tracks from 25 individuals tagged during 2014–2018.

The study estimated an approximately 30,000 km annual round trip between Beijing and semi-arid south-western Africa. It documented an autumn migration averaging about 14,733 km and a spring migration averaging about 13,572 km. The tracked birds used a migration corridor through Central Asia and Africa and spent the non-breeding period in southern Africa. The paper is open access and provides the primary research basis for the migration claim.

Research reference: https://doi.org/10.1186/s40462-022-00329-2

**Do not conflate the 2022 geolocator result with later continuous-flight claims.** A project update published by Wild Beijing reports that 41 birds were fitted with more advanced loggers, including accelerometers, in 2025 and that data retrieved in June 2026 indicated birds remained on the wing for nine months between leaving Beijing and returning. This is a significant project-reported result, but should be described as a recent tracking-project finding until its methods, sample size, analysis and peer-reviewed publication are independently available. The same project webpage gives a much larger estimate of annual distance travelled along the birds' meandering flight paths; do not present that estimate as equivalent to the 2022 peer-reviewed approximately 30,000 km migration-route estimate.

Project update: https://wildbeijing.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/swift-project-HOSTED.html

### 23.3 Urban nesting heritage and built-environment pressures

The Beijing Swift has a long association with the city's built environment. The Beijing Swift Project describes nesting in Beijing's historic gatehouses from at least 1417 and continued use of built structures. This is a valuable example of cultural and ecological heritage being intertwined, but the exact historical account should be attributed to the project unless corroborated by a separate archival or ornithological source.

Urban nesting habitat can be lost through demolition, renovation, roof replacement, sealing of cavities and building designs that do not provide suitable nest access. A 2019 Xinhua report documented a Beijing initiative to retrofit swift boxes and promote bird-friendly building design. It also described school students advocating directly to a property developer.

Reference: https://www.xinhuanet.com/english/2019-06/27/c_138178938.htm

The broader conservation literature supports protecting known nest sites and integrating appropriate nesting features into buildings. The British Trust for Ornithology notes that modern construction and refurbishment may remove nest sites, while also emphasizing that causes of population change can be complex and uncertain. Its recommendations support precautionary nest-site provision while research continues: https://www.bto.org/learn/about-birds/birdfacts/swift

**Important design caution:** Installing a nest box is not automatically a successful conservation outcome. A 2026 Beijing study of a swift tower reported that no Common Swifts occupied the boxes during the survey, while Eurasian Tree Sparrows occupied some of them; the authors attributed the failure to unsuitable design and placement. Nest structures must be designed for the target species, located appropriately and monitored for actual occupancy and breeding outcomes.

Reference: https://doi.org/10.5814/j.issn.1674-764x.2026.03.011

### 23.4 What the case teaches about urban conservation

The Beijing Swift case suggests a practical urban biodiversity approach:

- Identify species that use buildings, bridges, parks, wetlands, urban trees, watercourses and other city habitats.
- Map breeding and roosting locations with suitable seasonal survey methods and expert verification.
- Include biodiversity surveys early in demolition, renovation and new-building planning.
- Preserve confirmed nest access and habitat features wherever feasible; where impacts cannot be avoided, develop species-appropriate mitigation with qualified ecologists and the relevant authorities.
- Treat artificial nesting structures as an intervention to be tested and monitored, not as a proxy for conservation success.
- Record occupancy, breeding attempts, fledging success where feasible, maintenance condition and long-term persistence.
- Consider the wider ecological requirements of urban wildlife, including food availability, insect populations, water, vegetation, safe flight paths and connectivity.
- In migratory species programmes, consider breeding, non-breeding and stopover areas across the full annual cycle, and recognize that conservation action may require cooperation across countries.

A single-city nest-box project cannot by itself protect a migratory species across its entire range. It can, however, contribute to a wider evidence base, public awareness and international collaboration.

### 23.5 Public participation and conservation evidence

The Beijing Swift story demonstrates a useful engagement pathway: students learn about a local species, document its presence, communicate with building decision-makers and participate in habitat improvements. This is a valuable model for environmental education, provided that activities are designed with expert support and measurable ecological objectives.

STF should distinguish:
1. **Engagement outputs:** students trained, surveys conducted, sightings submitted, buildings assessed.
2. **Knowledge outcomes:** improved understanding of urban wildlife, migration and building-related habitat requirements.
3. **Behaviour and institutional outcomes:** nest sites retained, biodiversity provisions incorporated into designs, maintenance commitments made.
4. **Ecological outcomes:** verified nest occupancy, breeding success, survival or population trends where robust monitoring makes these measurable.

Do not report boxes installed, app downloads, sightings or school participation as proof that a bird population has recovered.

### 23.6 Relevance to India and Save Tiger First

The Beijing Swift is a case study, not a species template for India. The Beijing-breeding subspecies *Apus apus pekinensis* should not be presented as an Indian urban conservation target without authoritative evidence of its occurrence and relevance.

The transferable idea is to study the relationship between local wildlife and the built environment in Indian cities and towns. Potential STF work could include:

- City-specific baseline surveys of native birds and other urban wildlife.
- Documentation of nesting and roosting habitats in older buildings, trees and urban structures.
- Collaboration with local ornithologists, natural history groups, resident welfare associations, schools, architects, planners and municipal bodies.
- Biodiversity-sensitive building and renovation guidance based on locally occurring species.
- Citizen-science reporting with verification, seasonal context and clear data-quality standards.
- Monitoring before and after building interventions to determine whether habitat use and breeding outcomes actually improve.

Any Bengaluru or other Indian city programme should begin with local species inventories and expert advice. Do not copy Beijing nest-box dimensions, placement or seasonal timing for Indian species without species-specific evidence.

### 23.7 Potential future STF programme: Cities as Living Habitat

I recommend exploring a future cross-domain STF programme provisionally titled **Cities as Living Habitat**. It could connect biodiversity literacy, citizen science, built-environment planning and measurable urban conservation.

A responsible pilot would:
1. Select one city and a clearly defined neighbourhood or building cluster.
2. Establish a local species and habitat baseline with qualified partners.
3. Identify one or two locally relevant focal species and their actual ecological requirements.
4. Work with residents, schools and building owners to protect existing habitat before adding new structures.
5. Co-design interventions with species specialists and building professionals.
6. Establish monitoring and maintenance responsibilities before installation.
7. Measure ecological outcomes over multiple relevant seasons and publish limitations as well as successes.

This should remain a future programme concept. It is not part of Botanica Indica's initial product scope, and no nest-box or building intervention should be prescribed without local ecological assessment.

- **Indigenous stewardship and TEK:** knowledge rights, community authority, consent and fair benefits are fundamental to ethical conservation.
- **Urban biodiversity and migratory connectivity:** cities can be habitats and globally connected ecological places; built-environment decisions can have consequences far beyond city boundaries.

A proposed STF principle follows:

**Understand biodiversity across scales—from microbes to migratory species, from local habitats to global ecological connections—and turn that understanding into rights-respecting, evidence-based action with measurable outcomes.**

## 24. Art–science collaboration and ocean literacy: making hidden nature perceptible

### 24.1 The talk and its strategic contribution

In her TEDxYale talk, multidisciplinary artist Rebecca Rutstein explores collaboration between art and ocean science, drawing on research-vessel expeditions, deep-sea exploration and the translation of scientific observations into painting, sculpture and interactive installations.

Talk supplied for this review: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GglGhvJ4T0Y

The U.S. Department of State's Art in Embassies biography independently describes Rutstein's work at the intersection of art, science and technology, including collaborations with oceanographers, ecologists, microbiologists, molecular scientists and planetary geologists, seven sea expeditions and two deep-sea dives in the Alvin submersible. Her own biography also describes the use of visual and interactive experiences to make hidden environmental processes perceptible.

Sources:
- U.S. Department of State, Art in Embassies — Rebecca Rutstein: https://art.state.gov/personnel/rebecca_rutstein/
- National Endowment for the Arts, “At the Intersection of Art and Science: A Conversation with Rebecca Rutstein” (20 July 2023): https://www.arts.gov/stories/blog/2023/intersection-art-and-science-conversation-rebecca-rutstein
- Rebecca Rutstein, biography and artist statement: https://rebeccarutstein.com/bio-2019/

### 24.2 What the documented practice shows

In the National Endowment for the Arts interview, Rutstein describes beginning collaborations by listening, learning and understanding the scientist's research. She then selects patterns or phenomena for creative exploration; the exchange can also prompt scientists to reconsider or notice aspects of their own research. This is a reciprocal process, not merely an artist illustrating finished scientific conclusions.

The same interview describes *Shimmer* as a 64-foot interactive sculpture inspired by bioluminescent signals from deep-sea marine organisms. It also discusses a proposed immersive installation inspired by deep-sea coral forms. These are artistic interpretations informed by science; an artwork is not itself a scientific observation, ecological survey or validated restoration intervention.

### 24.3 Ocean oxygen: communicate the science accurately

NOAA estimates that roughly half of Earth's oxygen production comes from the ocean, largely through photosynthetic plankton, algae and some bacteria. NOAA also explains that approximately the same amount is consumed by marine organisms and decomposition. Avoid the oversimplified claim that the ocean supplies half of the oxygen humans breathe as a net contribution. Most oxygen humans breathe is atmospheric oxygen accumulated over geological time.

NOAA source: https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/ocean-oxygen.html

### 24.4 Ocean literacy as an educational framework

UNESCO's Intergovernmental Oceanographic Commission defines ocean literacy as understanding the ocean's influence on people and people's influence on the ocean. Its work connects understanding with communication, informed behaviour, education and co-designed solutions.

Sources:
- UNESCO-IOC Ocean Literacy: https://www.ioc.unesco.org/en/ocean-literacy
- UNESCO Ocean Literacy Portal: https://oceanliteracy.unesco.org/about/

This is relevant beyond coastal communities. Inland audiences influence ocean health through freshwater systems, watersheds, pollution, consumption, climate and policy. Any STF ocean-related education should explain these connections without implying that every inland activity has a direct or easily quantifiable marine impact.

### 24.5 What art–science collaboration may contribute

Potential contributions to STF include:
- Making spatial, temporal, microscopic, deep-sea or otherwise hidden ecological processes easier to perceive.
- Providing an accessible entry point to complex scientific subjects.
- Supporting curiosity, dialogue, reflection and interdisciplinary learning.
- Allowing artists and scientists to explore different questions using shared evidence.
- Communicating scale, uncertainty and interdependence through visual, sound, tactile or participatory media.

Art should complement scientific evidence, not replace it. Creative interpretation must be distinguished from measured data, scientific inference and metaphor.

### 24.6 Evidence limits: do not overclaim behaviour change

Chen and Mao's 2026 systematic review synthesised 97 peer-reviewed art–science collaboration publications from 2002–2024. The review found a growing but weakly consolidated field: qualitative and process-oriented case studies dominate, while explicit impact evaluations and cumulative outcome evidence are comparatively rare. It also identifies limited co-design of research questions, methods and evaluation by wider stakeholders.

This supports a careful position: artistic work can plausibly create engagement, wonder, dialogue and learning opportunities, but the existence of an artwork or an audience does not prove sustained behaviour change, policy influence or ecological recovery.

Reference:
- Chen, Y. & Mao, F. (2026), “Art-science collaboration for sustainability challenges: a systematic review of scholarship, sustainability framings, and stakeholder participation,” *Sustainability Science*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s11625-026-01903-y

### 24.7 Proposed STF practice: evidence-led creative interpretation

Explore an Art × Ecology × Science practice as a partnership method, not a new app or permanent programme.

A responsible project protocol should include:
1. A shared question agreed by artist, scientist and relevant community partners.
2. A scientific briefing that documents sources, methods, scale, uncertainty and limitations.
3. Creative independence and fair compensation for the artist.
4. Clear separation of measured data, scientific interpretation, artistic abstraction and metaphor.
5. Factual review of captions and explanatory claims by qualified scientists, without asking scientists to certify aesthetic meaning.
6. Written agreements covering copyright, licensing, reproduction, data/image permissions, attribution, compensation and reuse.
7. Community-led consent and governance wherever community knowledge, places or cultural expression are involved.
8. Accessible interpretation and relevant local-language material where feasible.
9. Evaluation proportionate to the project's aims: experience, dialogue, learning, behaviour and ecological outcomes are separate categories.
10. Consideration of material use, energy, travel, toxicity, waste and end-of-life impacts.

### 24.8 Indian relevance and potential partners

STF need not begin with a marine installation. Potential Indian themes include:
- Watershed connections between inland ecosystems, rivers, estuaries and coasts.
- Mangrove root systems, coastal sediment processes and nursery habitats.
- Soil microbial communities and nutrient cycling.
- Pollination, seed dispersal and plant–fungus relationships.
- Migratory bird routes and connections between urban and rural habitats.
- River health, wetlands, seasonal change and groundwater.

India's Blue Schools India network, part of the UNESCO-IOC Blue School Global Network, provides a current example of ocean literacy being connected to freshwater, climate, biodiversity, culture and livelihoods for learners regardless of where they live. Its UNESCO portal entry reports that India's national coordination was established in 2026 and lists participating schools, teachers and learners; these are programme participation figures, not evidence of ecological impact.

Reference: UNESCO Ocean Literacy Portal, India Blue School Network: https://oceanliteracy.unesco.org/projects/blue-school-global-network/blue-school-national-coordination-bodies/india

The National Centre for Coastal Research also describes opportunities for students to visit coastal science laboratories and interact with researchers, subject to institutional approval and scheduling:
https://www.nccr.gov.in/?q=node%2F1073

Any collaboration would require direct partner engagement; these references do not imply endorsement or an existing relationship with STF.

### 24.9 Modest pilot concept: Hidden Connections

A possible pilot, subject to approval, is **Hidden Connections: Art, Science and the Living World**.

Possible design:
- One defined ecological theme and one Indian study location or appropriately licensed dataset.
- One research partner and one or more paid artists.
- A co-designed visual, sound, sculpture, participatory or digital interpretation.
- A public exhibition or learning experience accompanied by a scientist-reviewed evidence guide.
- A facilitated dialogue or learning activity.
- Proportionate pre/post audience evaluation.
- A public project note documenting evidence, artistic interpretation, limitations, rights, environmental footprint and lessons.

Treat the first pilot as an experiment in science communication and interdisciplinary practice. Do not promise behaviour change or ecosystem improvement in advance.

### 24.10 Strategic synthesis

The Rutstein case adds a seventh dimension to the proposed STF conservation philosophy:

**Creative interpretation and wonder:** use art and sensory experience to help people perceive ecological processes and relationships that conventional communication may not make tangible, while preserving scientific accuracy, artistic independence and community rights.

This complements the six existing dimensions: NatureTech, human–nature connectedness, ecological interdependence, hidden biodiversity, Indigenous and local stewardship, and urban biodiversity.

### 24.11 Decision status

**Proposed practice and pilot concept — not an approved STF programme and not a claim that art alone produces conservation impact.**

Recommendation: explore one small, paid and co-designed Art × Ecology × Science pilot only after a scientific partner, artist, clear rights arrangements, audience plan, budget and proportionate evaluation method are secured. Do not add this as a core Botanica Indica product feature.


## 25. Indigenous-led systems change and institutional accountability

Marcia Turner's TEDxRRU talk, **“Indigenous Wisdom for Meaningful Change”** (17 July 2024), adds an institutional-governance dimension to STF's Indigenous stewardship principles: commitments to reconciliation, inclusion and decolonization are not meaningful if institutions do not change how power, authority and decisions operate.

The Royal Roads University description identifies four conditions in Turner's framework:
- **Trust**
- **Healing**
- **Self-determination**
- **Courage**

Turner describes courage as including the willingness to dismantle white supremacy and inequitable systems. The framework is rooted in her Gitxsan identity, ancestral teachings and professional work. It must be attributed to her and not presented as a generic Indigenous framework, universal doctrine or STF invention.  
Sources: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9WDIg_QWXWU and https://www.royalroads.ca/news/watch-2024-tedxrru-speakers

### 25.1 STF interpretation — institutional practice, not symbolic adoption

STF should treat this talk as a challenge to examine its own conduct, not as a framework to borrow decoratively. If STF works with Indigenous Peoples, Adivasi communities or other local communities in India, it must first understand the relevant local rights, identities, institutions and governance processes. Gitxsan concepts and conditions should not be assumed to map directly onto Indian contexts.

Possible institutional questions for STF:
- **Trust:** Are commitments, budgets, data uses, limitations and decisions transparent? Does STF do what it has promised? Is there a credible way to raise concerns and obtain remedy?
- **Healing:** Does the organisation understand historical and ongoing harms relevant to the place and people involved? Is there space for community-defined repair, or does STF rush to project delivery?
- **Self-determination:** Do relevant rights holders define priorities and hold real decision authority over participation, knowledge, data, methods, outputs and whether a project proceeds?
- **Courage:** Is STF prepared to change its own plans, budgets, timelines, ownership assumptions and public claims when community rights or ecological evidence require it?

These questions are an STF reflection tool inspired by the talk, not a substitute for the speaker's full framework or for community-defined processes.

### 25.2 Anti-performative safeguards

STF should not:
- use Indigenous imagery, stories, terminology or endorsements without authorization;
- claim a project is Indigenous-led when communities only provide consultation or data;
- treat a single individual as speaking for an entire people without a legitimate mandate;
- announce reconciliation, empowerment or decolonization outcomes based on attendance, workshops or memoranda alone;
- use an Indigenous framework as a brand asset while retaining unilateral control of decisions and data.

Where an Indigenous or local community partnership is proposed, the organisation should record who has authority to decide, what authority is delegated or retained, how concerns are resolved, and what resources enable participation.

### 25.3 Proposed governance indicators

Potential indicators include:
- proportion of relevant decisions made by or jointly with community-designated authorities;
- whether community-defined priorities materially changed project design or budgets;
- timely fulfilment of commitments and remedies for missed commitments;
- community assessment of trust, safety, influence and benefit;
- proportion of knowledge and data assets governed under agreed access and reuse rules;
- documented instances where a project was paused, redesigned or stopped in response to community decisions.

These are proposed indicators and should be co-designed. They must not become extractive scoring systems imposed on communities.

**Decision status:** Proposed institutional reflection and governance direction; not an approved STF policy or evidence of community endorsement.

## 26. India's ecological crisis, public accountability and ecological leadership

Two India-focused talks provide complementary lenses.

### 26.1 Public accountability and civic agency — Vimlendu Jha

Vimlendu Jha's talk, **“India's Environmental Crisis, Unspoken and Unheard”**, was delivered at TEDxJMC in March 2019. The official TED description frames the talk around land, water and air crises and argues that sustainability must move beyond academic discussion into practical action. The user-provided summary highlights urban lake fires, air pollution, infrastructure conflicts and the unequal burden placed on people who speak out.

Source: https://www.ted.com/talks/vimlendu_jha_india_s_environmental_crisis_unspoken_and_unheard

**Verification and caution:** This is a 2019 talk. Its examples and figures are historical claims from that period, not a current environmental status report. Specific statements about AQI maxima, lake conditions, proposed projects, tree numbers, water volumes or protest deaths must be checked against dated official records and reliable reporting before reuse. The talk's broad message—that recurring environmental harm can become normalized and that citizens with greater agency should participate in public accountability—is a strategic and civic argument, not a substitute for current monitoring data.

For Bengaluru, the Government of India's 2026 parliamentary response confirms the city is included as a non-attainment city under the National Clean Air Programme and describes a city action plan targeting sources including road dust, construction and demolition activities and transport-related emissions. This is a more appropriate current policy reference than repeating older AQI claims from the 2019 talk.  
Source: https://sansad.in/getFile/loksabhaquestions/annex/187/AU2639_1pjHt4.pdf?source=pqals

**STF implication:** Consider urban ecological accountability a legitimate programme area, but work through documented evidence, lawful civic participation, transparent methods and credible local partnerships. STF should not make unverified allegations, publish uncontextualized pollution claims or expose individual contributors to avoidable risk.

### 26.2 Forests, medicines and human survival — Srinivasulu IFS

The TEDxABBS talk **“Forest Conservation is Conversation on Life”** was published on 25 September 2025. Its official description identifies Srinivasulu as a senior Indian Forest Service officer and presents forests as repositories of biodiversity, medicine, food security and genetic resources. It connects conservation to human survival and dignity rather than limiting it to charismatic species or tree cover.

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EYugido3Fu0

The broad proposition is well founded: plant and microbial diversity has contributed to medicines and other useful compounds. But examples need botanical and geographic precision. Quinine is associated with *Cinchona* species native to the Americas; paclitaxel (Taxol) was first isolated from Pacific yew (*Taxus brevifolia*) in North America; vincristine derives from *Catharanthus roseus*, native to Madagascar; and digoxin is derived from *Digitalis* species. These examples demonstrate the value of biodiversity and natural-products research, but they are not all examples of medicines originating from Indian forests.  
Sources: https://www.cancer.gov/research/progress/discovery/taxol ; https://www.kew.org/plants/pacific-yew ; https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36410328/ ; https://www.nejm.org/doi/10.1056/NEJM199412083312306

The talk's broad species-count statement should not be presented as a settled count of known species or as a verified inventory of species interacting in forests. Likewise, the Green Revolution reference should be explained through crop genetic resources and crop wild relatives, with crop-specific evidence rather than a blanket claim.

**India relevance:** The Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change's 2026 World Wildlife Day backgrounder reports approximately 15,000 medicinal plant species in India and describes conservation measures including Medicinal Plants Conservation Areas and ex-situ accessions. These are government-reported figures and programme descriptions; they should be attributed to the source and not treated as a substitute for species-level assessment.  
Source: https://www.pib.gov.in/PressNoteDetails.aspx?ModuleId=3&NoteId=157632&lang=1&reg=3

**STF implication:** Conservation communication should connect biodiversity with livelihoods, public health, food systems, cultural knowledge and ecological resilience—without reducing nature to a pharmacy or implying that species are valuable only when they have commercial or medical utility.

### 26.3 A proposed India-first accountability and stewardship approach

STF may explore small, evidence-led projects that:
1. document a clearly defined local ecological problem and its history;
2. identify the responsible public authority and relevant legal or planning process;
3. use reliable baseline data and disclose limitations;
4. include affected communities and local knowledge holders in defining the problem and response;
5. distinguish awareness, advocacy, monitoring, restoration and enforcement as different activities;
6. protect the safety and privacy of community members and environmental defenders;
7. report both progress and failure transparently.

Potential topics include urban lakes and wetlands, native urban trees, habitat connectivity, soil health, medicinal plant conservation and protection of ecologically sensitive landscapes. These are candidate themes, not approved projects.

**Decision status:** Proposed programme direction; no specific campaign, litigation, restoration intervention or monitoring project is approved.

## 27. Zoos, aquariums and ex-situ conservation: a qualified role

Christoph Kiessling's TEDxSOAS talk, **“Respect for the environment: Protection and Conservation”** (April 2018), argues that modern zoos and aquariums can contribute to conservation through education, research, species management and public engagement. The talk also uses population growth, habitat loss and pollution to illustrate pressures on biodiversity.

Source: https://www.ted.com/talks/christoph_kiessling_respect_for_the_environment_protection_and_conservation

The strategic value of this talk is the invitation to consider the full conservation toolkit, including ex-situ management. However, its claims about population growth, plastic entering the ocean and species counts per hectare are dated or lack sufficient context in the supplied summary. They should not be repeated as current global statistics.

### 27.1 Independent conservation verification

The IUCN Species Survival Commission's 2023 position statement recognizes that well-run botanic gardens, aquariums and zoos can contribute to species conservation through ex-situ and in-situ management, research, education, community engagement, policy, funding and species recovery. It also explicitly recognizes that not all institutions meet their conservation potential and identifies risks from poor population management, disease management, unsuitable releases and unapproved wild collection.

IUCN's position supports a **One Plan Approach**: connect ex-situ and in-situ conservation rather than treating captive populations as a replacement for protecting habitats and wild populations.  
Source: https://iucn.org/sites/default/files/2023-10/2023-position-statement-on-the-role-of-botanic-gardens-aquariums-and-zoos-in-species-conservation.pdf

### 27.2 STF's position

STF should neither endorse nor dismiss zoos and aquariums as a category. Any partnership should be assessed institution by institution, species by species and programme by programme.

Due diligence should consider:
- relevant national legal permissions, recognition and accreditation;
- animal welfare, veterinary capacity, husbandry and population-management standards;
- transparent conservation objectives and measurable contribution to wild populations or habitat protection;
- scientific basis and risk assessment for breeding, release or translocation;
- provenance and legality of animals and biological material;
- public education quality, research integrity and community relationships;
- independent reporting and willingness to disclose adverse outcomes.

**No-go principle:** STF should not promote captive display as equivalent to habitat conservation, endorse a release without a scientifically justified and authorized conservation plan, or use the “Noah's Ark” metaphor to imply that ex-situ collections can preserve ecosystem function or replace wild habitats.

**Decision status:** Proposed partnership-screening principle; not an endorsement of any named zoo, aquarium or wildlife facility.
