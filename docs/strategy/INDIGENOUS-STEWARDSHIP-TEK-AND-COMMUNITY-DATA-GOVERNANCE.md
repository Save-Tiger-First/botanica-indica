## 1. Executive recommendation

STF should treat Indigenous stewardship, Traditional Ecological Knowledge (TEK), community authority and Indigenous data governance as cross-cutting requirements for future conservation programmes and community-facing data systems.

The central strategic question is not only what biodiversity knowledge can be collected, but **who holds that knowledge, who has authority to share it, who determines its permitted uses, and who benefits from conservation and technology activities**.

Technology should support community-defined priorities and legitimate governance processes. It must not turn communities into unpaid data suppliers, transfer authority to an external platform, or make restricted knowledge public by default.

This addendum is a strategic proposal. It does not claim endorsement by any Indigenous Peoples or local communities.

## 2. Talk and independent verification

The official TEDx Talks publication identifies the presentation as “Guardians of Life: Indigenous Wisdom, Science and Restoring the Planet”, delivered by National Geographic photographer Kiliii Yuyan at TEDxBerkeley and published on 22 April 2025. The talk uses examples from Indigenous communities, including Palau and the Amazon.

### 2.1 The 40% statistic — correct the wording

Garnett et al. (2018), “A spatial overview of the global importance of Indigenous lands for conservation”, estimated that Indigenous Peoples manage or have tenure rights over at least 38 million km² in 87 countries or politically distinct areas. The study reported that these lands intersect about 40% of terrestrial protected areas and ecologically intact landscapes.

This does **not** mean Indigenous territories comprise 40% of all remaining natural lands. Nor does it establish a universal comparative ranking showing that Indigenous territories always have higher biodiversity than government-managed national parks. The defensible conclusion is that Indigenous lands overlap a substantial share of globally important protected and ecologically intact areas, and that recognizing rights, institutions and benefit sharing is important to conservation.

Source: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41893-018-0100-6

### 2.2 Traditional knowledge and international biodiversity policy

Article 8(j) of the Convention on Biological Diversity calls for respect, preservation and maintenance of relevant knowledge, innovations and practices of Indigenous and local communities. It links wider application to the approval and involvement of knowledge holders and equitable benefit sharing.

The Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework reinforces related principles:
- Target 3 recognizes Indigenous and traditional territories and requires respect for the rights of Indigenous Peoples and local communities.
- Target 21 says traditional knowledge, innovations, practices and technologies should only be accessed with free, prior and informed consent, in accordance with national legislation.
- Target 22 calls for full, equitable, inclusive and effective participation and respect for rights over lands, territories, resources and traditional knowledge.

Sources:
- https://www.cbd.int/traditional
- https://www.cbd.int/article/cop15-final-text-kunming-montreal-gbf-221222

### 2.3 Examples in the talk: evidence and qualifications

**Palau — Bul.** The talk presents Bul as a customary system through which traditional authorities can restrict fishing in selected reef areas in response to ecological conditions. This is a useful example of customary marine governance and adaptive stewardship. Before STF uses it as an operational model or makes claims about measured fish-stock recovery, it should seek Palauan community-authorized sources and fisheries research covering local authority, ecological indicators, compliance and outcomes. Do not assume the term or institution can be transplanted elsewhere.

**Australia — cultural burning.** Bird et al. (2008) studied Aboriginal burning and landscape mosaics in Australian arid environments. Their research found greater diversity of successional stages in anthropogenic landscapes than under the studied lightning-fire regime. More recent work describes sophisticated Indigenous fire management in tropical savannas, including management of fuel loads, fire connectivity and vegetation structure. These findings do not justify a universal prescription to burn. Practice depends on local ecology, cultural authority, season, objectives and practitioner knowledge.

Sources:
- https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0804757105
- https://www.nature.com/articles/s41561-024-01388-3

**Greenland — narwhal hunting.** The talk's summary describes traditional hunting without motorized boats and rifles. This must not be generalized. Specialist sources describe regional variation: kayak-based approaches remain in some parts of Northwest Greenland, while small motorboats and rifles are used in several other hunting contexts. The example should be presented as geographically and historically situated, not as a universal description of Inuit hunting or proof that one method guarantees population sustainability.

Sources:
- https://nammco.no/narwhal/
- https://nammco.no/overview-of-marine-mammal-hunting-methods-inc-regulations-monitoring-observation-in-nammco-member-countries/
- https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0308597X24003993

**Klamath River — dam removal and tribal leadership.** Four lower Klamath hydroelectric dams were removed between 2023 and October 2024. California's 2 October 2024 announcement identifies tribal leaders, including the Karuk and Yurok Tribes, as central participants in the restoration effort. Dam removal restored fish access to more than 400 miles of habitat, while restoration of formerly inundated lands and longer-term ecological recovery continue. Completion of dam removal is not equivalent to complete ecosystem recovery.

Sources:
- https://www.gov.ca.gov/2024/10/02/klamath-river-dams-fully-removed-ahead-of-schedule/
- https://klamathrenewal.org/the-project/


### 2.4 Marcia Turner — Four Indigenous Conditions of Systems Change

Marcia Turner's talk, **“Indigenous Wisdom for Meaningful Change”**, was published by TEDx Talks on 17 July 2024. Royal Roads University's 2024 TEDxRRU speaker page identifies the four conditions in her framework as **trust, healing, self-determination and courage**. The university's description also records her emphasis on courage to dismantle white supremacy and the hard work of decolonization.

Sources:
- https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9WDIg_QWXWU
- https://www.royalroads.ca/news/watch-2024-tedxrru-speakers
- Marcia Turner's own announcement describing the framework as the “Four Indigenous Conditions of Systems Change”: https://www.linkedin.com/posts/marcia-turner-dhnb_indigenous-wisdom-for-meaningful-change-activity-7219763836206075904

**Interpretation for STF:** This framework adds a systems-change test to the practical safeguards in this document. Trust requires transparent commitments and accountability; healing requires acknowledging harms and allowing community-defined repair; self-determination requires real authority over priorities and decisions; courage requires institutions to change their own practices and redistribute decision power where appropriate.

These explanations are an STF reflection aid, not a substitute for Turner's full framework. The framework is rooted in Turner's Gitxsan identity, ancestral teachings and professional work. STF must attribute it accurately and must not present it as a universal Indigenous framework, a generic checklist, or an STF invention. Gitxsan concepts and governance processes must not be presumed to apply to Adivasi communities or other peoples in India. The relevant people and rights holders define their own context, institutions and decision processes.

**Anti-tokenism test:** STF should not cite the four conditions in a public statement while retaining unilateral control over project goals, budgets, data, interpretation and publication. If the organisation invokes the framework, it should be willing to demonstrate how trust, healing, self-determination and courage have changed its actual decisions and conduct.

### 2.5 Albert Wiggan — recognizing Indigenous knowledge as science

Albert Wiggan's TEDxSydney talk, **“The case to recognise Indigenous knowledge as science”**, was published on 15 July 2019 (the TED listing itself gives May 2019; TEDx/YouTube publication records give 15 July 2019). The official TEDx description identifies him as a Bardi-Kija-Nyul Nyul man from the Dampier Peninsula, working with Indigenous environmental and community organisations. He argues for recognition of Indigenous knowledge as science rather than relegating it to a lesser category.

Source: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=X5QON5l6zy8

The talk challenges a hierarchy in which Indigenous knowledge is treated only as anecdote or raw material for external scientific validation. Its strategic contribution is the case for recognizing Indigenous knowledge systems as knowledge systems with their own methods, institutions, custodians, histories and standards.

**Important qualification:** Recognition does not require collapsing Indigenous knowledge and academic science into one method or vocabulary. Nor should STF assume all knowledge holders use the same approach to observation, experimentation, evidence or transmission. The proper approach is respectful collaboration where invited, with clear authority, attribution, consent, context and reciprocal benefit.

A peer-reviewed example of collaborative practice is Lindsay et al. (2022), which describes integrating scientific and Aboriginal knowledge, practice and priorities to conserve an endangered rainforest ecosystem in the Kimberley. It is an example from a specific place and partnership, not a template that can be transplanted without local authority and adaptation.  
Source: https://doi.org/10.1111/emr.12535

**STF implication:** In appropriate projects, community-defined knowledge and scientific evidence can be brought into dialogue without requiring one to erase or subordinate the other. The organisation must preserve provenance and context, identify who has authority to interpret or share knowledge, and avoid claiming ownership over knowledge that communities hold.


## 3. Critical safeguards: do not romanticize, homogenize or extract

1. **Do not homogenize Indigenous Peoples.** Indigenous Peoples and local communities are diverse, contemporary societies with distinct histories, rights, governance systems, priorities and relationships with nature. TEK is dynamic and can coexist with scientific research, modern tools and changing livelihoods.
2. **Do not treat TEK as a free public resource.** Knowledge may be sacred, restricted, seasonal, gender-specific, family-held or community-held. Public visibility of a story, image, practice or species location does not automatically grant permission to record, digitize, publish, commercialize, redistribute or use it for AI training.
3. **Consent is an ongoing governance relationship.** Relevant knowledge holders and legitimate community processes must determine whether and how engagement proceeds. Agreements should specify purpose, audiences, access, retention, onward sharing, attribution, benefit sharing and review or withdrawal arrangements, subject to applicable law.
4. **Do not reduce TEK to scientific validation.** Community knowledge and scientific research may use different concepts, evidence practices, temporal scales and criteria. Preserve the knowledge holder's interpretation, context and provenance; scientific review must not override community authority.
5. **Do not equate consultation with power.** Community leadership means meaningful influence over objectives, governance, methods, monitoring, interpretation, publication, benefits and decisions to stop or change a project.
6. **Do not assume all customary practices are automatically sustainable in every current context.** Evaluate specific practices with the relevant knowledge holders and ecological evidence, without dismissing or romanticizing them.
7. **Do not use Indigenous frameworks as institutional decoration.** If STF invokes a framework such as Turner's four conditions, it must examine how the framework changes its own governance, resource allocation, decision rights and accountability; accurate attribution and local relevance are essential.

## 4. Indigenous data governance: CARE alongside FAIR

The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance complement conventional FAIR data principles:

- **Collective Benefit:** data systems should enable collective benefit defined by the relevant Indigenous Peoples.
- **Authority to Control:** recognize and empower their authority over data concerning their peoples, lands, resources and knowledge.
- **Responsibility:** data users are accountable for supporting self-determination and explaining how data are used.
- **Ethics:** rights, wellbeing, justice and prevention of harm guide the data lifecycle.

Reference: Carroll et al. (2020), “The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance”, *Data Science Journal* 19(1):43. https://doi.org/10.5334/dsj-2020-043

For STF, future systems should be able to support:

- community-defined governance authorities and knowledge-holder roles;
- granular permissions for collection, storage, access, display, translation, publication, research reuse, commercial use and AI/ML training;
- community-defined categories for public, community-controlled, restricted, confidential, sacred or prohibited knowledge;
- generalization or withholding of culturally sensitive locations and resources;
- attribution preferences, collective attribution, anonymity or non-attribution;
- agreed benefit-sharing terms and community access to copies and results;
- provenance that records authority and permitted conditions without exposing restricted content;
- separation of consent to participate from permission to publish, redistribute, commercialize or train models;
- human review and community governance of translations, summaries and AI-generated interpretations;
- security, access logging, retention controls and a response process for unauthorized disclosure.

CARE is not a substitute for law, community protocols, agreements or sovereignty. A single generic consent form or database role cannot represent every community's governance system. Open-data defaults are inappropriate for some community-related information.

## 5. India-specific implications

Global examples must not be copied directly into India. India has distinct legal categories, constitutional arrangements, community institutions and statutory frameworks. STF should not use “Indigenous”, “Adivasi”, “Scheduled Tribe”, “forest-dwelling Scheduled Tribe”, “Other Traditional Forest Dweller” and “local community” as interchangeable labels. Appropriate terminology, rights holders and decision-making authority must be established in each context, respecting self-identification and applicable law.

The Forest Rights Act, 2006 recognizes individual and community forest rights. Section 5 empowers holders of forest rights, Gram Sabhas and village-level institutions to protect wildlife, forests and biodiversity and to regulate access to community forest resources in accordance with the Act. The Ministry of Tribal Affairs describes community rights that include access to biodiversity, community rights to intellectual property and traditional knowledge, and rights to protect, regenerate, conserve or manage community forest resources for sustainable use.

Sources:
- https://tribal.nic.in/FRA.aspx
- https://hpforest.gov.in/storage/files/4/Acts/Forest%20Right%20Act%2C2006%20--%20actsfile15-07-2020-1594873226.pdf

The Biological Diversity Act, 2002, as amended, and its current subordinate instruments are also relevant where biological resources and associated knowledge are accessed or utilized. The National Biodiversity Authority reports that the Biological Diversity Rules, 2024 came into force on 21 December 2024 and were amended in 2025; it also lists the 2025 Access and Benefit Sharing Regulations. Applicability depends on the actors, resources, purpose, activity and statutory exemptions. STF should obtain current Indian legal advice before collecting, transferring, researching, commercializing or sharing biological resources, associated traditional knowledge or related digital sequence information.

Sources:
- https://nbaindia.nic.in/about-us/faqs
- https://www.nbaindia.nic.in/public-information/notification-guidelines

This is a strategic design requirement, not a legal opinion. It does not assume every educational or citizen-science activity triggers the same statutory approval or benefit-sharing requirements.

## 6. Recommended STF commitments

- Build relationships before designing data collection; do not approach communities only as sources of photographs, observations, stories or local names.
- Identify appropriate community institutions, rights holders and knowledge holders with the community itself; do not appoint representatives on its behalf.
- Budget for participation, translation, travel, training, community monitoring and local capacity.
- Co-design objectives and indicators. Community-defined outcomes may include livelihood security, cultural continuity, customary use, language, habitat condition and species recovery.
- Recognize community monitoring and stewardship as conservation work, not merely as data acquisition for STF.
- Examine STF's own institutional behaviour through trust, healing, self-determination and courage when it invokes Marcia Turner's framework; document changed decisions rather than symbolic statements.
- Recognize Indigenous knowledge systems as knowledge systems in their own right, and create respectful, consent-based collaboration with scientific institutions only where the relevant knowledge holders wish to do so.
- Return useful outputs in locally appropriate languages and formats and provide communities access to their own records and agreed results.
- Support community control over sensitive knowledge, place names, locations, images, recordings and ecological observations.
- Where appropriate, support partnerships with universities, botanists, ecologists and government bodies under agreed governance and benefit-sharing arrangements.
- Measure whether programmes strengthen community authority and produce ecological and social outcomes. The number of records collected is not a proxy for empowerment or conservation success.

## 7. Relevance to Botanica Indica — and the boundary

Botanica Indica may eventually help document culturally significant plants, local plant names, uses, habitat knowledge, seasonal observations and community-led conservation practices. This is not permission to build a public ethnobotanical knowledge database by default.

For the initial product:

- Keep the core focus on evidence-backed plant identification and botanical information.
- Do not solicit traditional medicinal, ritual, sacred or restricted knowledge as ordinary crowdsourced content.
- Do not publish precise locations of culturally sensitive plants, harvesting areas or sacred sites without explicit authorization and risk review.
- Do not train identification models on community-contributed images or associated knowledge beyond the permissions actually granted.
- Separate botanical facts from public scientific references from community-held knowledge and locally governed observations.
- Require a separate co-design process, rights and legal assessment, governance model, benefit-sharing approach and community-approved pilot before proposing a community knowledge module.

## 8. Strategic synthesis across the seven talks

Together, the five talks suggest a more complete STF approach:

- **NatureTech:** use technology to support nature rather than treating nature as a passive resource.
- **Human–nature connectedness:** strengthen the understanding that people are part of ecological systems.
- **Forest interdependence:** recognize ecological relationships while distinguishing demonstrated mechanisms from popular simplifications.
- **Hidden microbial biodiversity:** include below-ground life and ecosystem processes in the meaning and measurement of biodiversity.
- **Indigenous stewardship and TEK:** recognize rights, authority, knowledge systems and community leadership as central to equitable conservation.
- **Indigenous-led systems change:** trust, healing, self-determination and courage challenge institutions to move beyond performative commitments and change their actual distribution of power.
- **Indigenous knowledge as science:** respect Indigenous knowledge systems on their own terms and enable ethical collaboration without collapsing different knowledge traditions into one hierarchy.

Proposed STF principle:

**Conservation technology must serve living ecosystems and the people who sustain relationships with them. It should strengthen community authority, protect knowledge rights, share benefits fairly and support evidence-based ecological recovery.**

This is a proposed strategic interpretation, not an approved organizational policy or a substitute for engagement with Indigenous Peoples and local communities.

## 10. Decision status

**Proposed strategic and governance principles — not an approved STF policy, community agreement, legal opinion or operational conservation protocol.**

Recommendation: incorporate Indigenous stewardship, TEK and Indigenous data governance into STF's strategy as cross-cutting requirements for future community-facing programmes and data systems. Do not collect or publish community-held knowledge until appropriate rights holders and governance processes have determined whether, how and under what conditions this is permitted.


## 11. Readiness gate before any STF community knowledge programme

This section converts the strategic safeguards above into a practical go/no-go review. Completing a checklist does not replace community authorization, legal advice or local protocols.

| Gate | Evidence required | Accountable review |
|---|---|---|
| Context and rights-holder identification | Community-led identification of relevant peoples, institutions, knowledge holders, rights and decision processes; terminology validated locally | Community-designated representatives and qualified local advisers |
| Purpose and necessity | Written explanation of the conservation need, why knowledge collection is necessary, alternatives that avoid collection, and who benefits | STF programme lead with community-designated reviewers |
| Consent and authorization | Documented free, prior and informed consent or other applicable approval/ involvement process, in locally appropriate language and form; scope and limits explicit | Relevant rights holders and legitimate community processes |
| Knowledge classification | Community-defined public, controlled, restricted, sacred, confidential or prohibited categories; no default-public assumption | Knowledge holders / community authority |
| Data and IP rights | Rights assessment for text, images, recordings, names, locations, biological resources and derived data; permissions separated by use | Legal adviser and community-designated authority |
| Access and security | Role and purpose-based access, least privilege, audit logs, retention/deletion arrangements, sensitive-location controls and incident response | Data protection/security lead with community review |
| AI and downstream use | Explicit decisions on model training, embeddings, translation, summarization, model outputs, third-party processors, redistribution and commercial use | Community authority, technical lead and legal adviser |
| Benefit sharing and reciprocity | Agreed financial/non-financial benefits, participation compensation, capacity support, access to outputs and attribution preferences | Community-designated authority and STF leadership |
| Interpretation and publication | Community review of context, translations, maps, summaries, public communications and any claims attributed to the community | Knowledge holders / designated reviewers |
| Monitoring and grievance | Community-defined success indicators, accessible complaint channel, periodic review, pause/withdrawal process and remedy for misuse | Jointly agreed governance body |
| Close-out and continuity | Agreed return, archival, transfer, deletion or continued stewardship of data; responsibilities after project funding ends | Community authority and STF programme owner |

### Minimum no-go conditions

STF should not collect, digitize, publish, share or use community-held knowledge for AI/ML if any of the following applies:

- the relevant rights holders or legitimate decision-making process have not been identified with the community;
- consent or authorization is absent, unclear, coerced, out of date or does not cover the proposed use;
- the knowledge is restricted, sacred or otherwise not authorized for the intended audience or purpose;
- rights to images, recordings, biological resources, associated knowledge or third-party materials are unresolved;
- downstream access, model training, onward transfer or commercial use is not covered by the agreed permissions;
- disclosure creates an unmitigated risk to people, cultural heritage, species, harvesting sites or territories;
- the community cannot meaningfully participate in governance or access agreed benefits and outputs.

A no-go decision may mean redesigning the programme so that it does not collect the knowledge. It must not be treated as a problem to overcome through broader consent wording.

### Review sequence

1. Conduct a desk-based legal and ecological scoping exercise without collecting community-held knowledge.
2. Identify potential community partners through appropriate, trusted channels; do not assume a single individual speaks for an entire community.
3. Ask whether the community wishes to explore the proposal and allow it to define its own decision process.
4. Co-design purpose, methods, governance, permissions, benefits and indicators before any collection.
5. Obtain legal review of the applicable Indian and other relevant legal frameworks, including the current Biological Diversity Act and rules where relevant, the Forest Rights Act where applicable, privacy and intellectual-property obligations, and any local/community protocols.
6. Pilot only with explicit authorization and a jointly agreed monitoring and grievance mechanism.
7. Review outcomes with the community before scaling, publishing or reusing any material.

**No programme should proceed merely because this document exists or because STF has adopted internal procedures. Community authorization is specific to the relevant people, place, knowledge and proposed use.**
