# STF Bengaluru Urban Biodiversity Feasibility Pilot
## A 12-week, evidence-led baseline and community learning pilot

**Document ID:** STF-PILOT-BLR-001  
**Version:** 0.1 — proposed for review  
**Prepared:** 16 November 2026  
**Status:** Proposal only — not an approved programme, budget, partnership, field activity or conservation intervention  
**Proposed duration:** 12 weeks after all approval gates are passed  
**Proposed budget ceiling:** ₹4,00,000 (indicative planning estimate; subject to quotes and formal approval)  
**Proposed geography:** One public urban green-blue site in the Greater Bengaluru Area, to be selected through due diligence  
**Accountable owner:** To be appointed by STF's authorized governing body  
**Scientific lead:** Qualified urban ecologist/botanist to be appointed  
**Proposed decision:** Authorize a time-limited feasibility and baseline pilot only if all Gate 0 requirements are satisfied

## 1. Executive recommendation

Save Tiger First (STF) should test a small, scientifically supervised **urban biodiversity baseline and community learning pilot** at one carefully selected public green-blue site in the Greater Bengaluru Area.

The pilot should document a limited set of observable biodiversity and habitat indicators, establish a transparent baseline, test a safe and repeatable citizen-observation process, and identify whether a longer partnership programme is justified.

**The pilot is not a lake-restoration project.** It will not desilt, alter water flows, divert sewage, construct wetlands, plant trees, remove vegetation, handle wildlife, collect biological specimens, or undertake physical habitat modification.

The proposed focal groups are:

1. Native and naturalised vascular plants, with particular attention to ground-layer and non-tree plants that may not be adequately represented in a tree inventory.
2. Common birds, recorded through a consistent, expert-designed observation protocol.
3. Butterflies, recorded through a consistent, expert-designed transect or observation protocol.

The site assessment will also document broad habitat features, disturbance pressures visible from authorized public areas, and connectivity context. It will not claim to measure all biodiversity, ecosystem services, water quality, pollinator health, or ecological recovery.

The pilot should be designed to complement existing city work and data—not duplicate it. It should use the Bengaluru Urban Forest Manual and available public tree-census information as reference inputs, while recognizing existing lake-maintenance and lake-health programmes. It should not create a new app or a parallel lake dashboard.

**My recommendation:** approve this as a capped feasibility exercise, not as a public-facing conservation campaign. The first success criterion is whether STF can produce a credible, independently reviewed baseline with permissions, local legitimacy and a realistic continuation plan.

## 2. Why this pilot is worth testing

### 2.1 Bengaluru has relevant urban biodiversity frameworks

The Bengaluru Urban Forest Manual (2025) includes an urban biodiversity indicator framework with 23 indicators across native biodiversity, ecosystem services, and governance/management. It identifies native plants, birds and butterflies among the biodiversity indicators and emphasizes baseline assessment and periodic monitoring. The Manual is a useful city-specific technical reference. Its legal status, current adoption and the responsible authority for any particular site must be confirmed before it is treated as binding for that site.

Reference: Bengaluru Urban Forest Manual 2025, Chapter 4.3, pp. 34–36:  
https://apps.bbmp.gov.in/bcap/PDF/Forest%20Manual%20-%20English%20pdf%2001072025.pdf

The Singapore Index on Cities' Biodiversity is another established city self-assessment tool. It has 28 indicators and is designed to compare a city's performance with its own baseline over time. STF should learn from its baseline logic, but must not claim to have conducted a City Biodiversity Index assessment through this small site-level pilot.

Reference: Singapore National Parks Board, Singapore Index on Cities' Biodiversity:  
https://www.nparks.gov.sg/nature/singapore-index-cities-biodiversity

### 2.2 Existing work creates an opportunity—and a duplication risk

BBMP's 2024–25 performance report described the Kere Mitra mobile/web facility through which citizens could submit lake inspection reports and complaints. This was primarily a lake-maintenance and civic-monitoring mechanism, not a substitute for a scientifically designed biodiversity survey.

Reference: BBMP Performance Report 2024–25, lake management section:  
https://updates.bbmpgov.in/v1/api/file/489640621543-ATR%20Final%20Copy-English.pdf

ATREE's 2024–25 annual report describes LakeRevive, a decision-support tool developed for lake restoration that integrates hydrology, water quality, biodiversity and socio-economic considerations, with context-specific indicators and implementation guidance. STF should not build a competing lake-health application or claim to fill a gap that is already being addressed by qualified local organisations.

Reference: ATREE Annual Report 2024–25, “Restoration and co-management of a peri-urban lake in Bengaluru”:  
https://www.atree.org/sites/default/files/reports/AR-2024-25.pdf

A 2026 synthesis of 413 academic, policy and practitioner documents on Bengaluru's lakes highlights interconnected ecological, institutional and social pressures, fragmented governance, and the possibility that technical responses can create unintended stressors. A separate 2026 study of eight Bengaluru lakes reports trade-offs from inlet-focused interventions. These findings strongly support a non-intervention baseline pilot rather than STF attempting physical lake works.

References:
- Bergman et al. (2026), “From Drivers to Responses: Local Insights and National Frameworks for Restoring Urban Lakes in Bengaluru”, *Water*, 18(10), 1168: https://doi.org/10.3390/w18101168
- “Evaluating the effectiveness of inlet-focused interventions on the water quality and ecosystem services of urban lakes in the tropics” (2026), *Frontiers in Water*: https://doi.org/10.3389/frwa.2026.1857439

### 2.3 The pilot can connect to Botanica Indica without making Botanica Indica responsible for conservation delivery

The plant component can test a disciplined workflow for image-supported botanical observations: observation context, provisional identification, expert review, taxonomic source, confidence, evidence quality and unresolved records.

However:
- Botanica Indica is not assumed to be production-ready.
- Pilot data must not be uploaded into the product until the product's data rights, scientific provenance, taxonomy and privacy controls are ready.
- The pilot must not use AI-generated identifications as verified species records.
- A record that cannot be identified reliably must remain unresolved rather than being forced into a species name.

The pilot is therefore a potential learning input for Botanica Indica, not a product launch or a substitute for the scientific data-model review.

## 3. Objectives and research questions

### 3.1 Objectives

1. Select one site through a transparent ecological, legal, governance and feasibility scorecard.
2. Produce a limited, repeatable baseline for plants, birds and butterflies at that site.
3. Document habitat types, broad disturbance pressures and the immediate green-blue context.
4. Test whether trained volunteers can contribute useful observations under expert supervision.
5. Test a consent-based, accessible public learning activity in English and Kannada where appropriate.
6. Identify data gaps, partner needs, legal questions, risks and realistic maintenance requirements.
7. Make an evidence-based go / redesign / pause / stop recommendation for any longer programme.

### 3.2 Research questions

- Which plant, bird and butterfly taxa can be reliably documented at the selected site during the pilot period?
- Which habitat features appear to support the recorded taxa?
- What important evidence gaps or identification uncertainties remain?
- Can volunteers collect observations consistently after training and quality checks?
- What existing city datasets and programmes can be reused?
- What would be required to conduct a meaningful seasonal or multi-year assessment?
- Is there a credible partner and governance arrangement for continuation?

### 3.3 What the pilot cannot answer

A 12-week pilot cannot establish long-term population trends, prove that biodiversity has increased, attribute ecological change to STF, establish city-wide biodiversity status, or demonstrate the success of a restoration intervention. Seasonal variation and detectability must be acknowledged.

## 4. Scope boundaries

### In scope

- One public site and a clearly mapped, authorized survey boundary.
- A short desk review of available site history, public maps, land/custody information and existing biodiversity records.
- Expert-designed plant, bird and butterfly observation protocols.
- Three or more repeat survey rounds where access, weather and expert advice permit.
- Photo-based documentation without specimen collection.
- Expert verification of records, with uncertainty retained.
- Habitat and disturbance observations from authorized locations.
- One or two local learning / volunteer orientation sessions.
- A final baseline report, data register, limitations statement and continuation recommendation.

### Explicitly out of scope

- Lake rejuvenation, desilting, sewage diversion, constructed wetlands or water engineering.
- Tree planting, landscaping, pruning, invasive plant removal or habitat modification.
- Wildlife capture, handling, feeding, tagging, nest approach or disturbance.
- Collection, removal, transport or laboratory testing of biological specimens.
- eDNA, DNA sequencing, microbiome or soil sampling.
- Water-quality testing in this first pilot.
- Collection of Indigenous, Adivasi, customary or other traditional ecological knowledge.
- Accessing restricted, private or culturally sensitive locations.
- Building a mobile app, public dashboard or new observation platform.
- Claims of carbon sequestration, ecosystem-service improvement, pollination improvement or conservation impact.

Any proposal to add an excluded activity requires a separate scientific, legal, budgetary and governance review and a new approval.

## 5. Site selection — no site is pre-approved

STF should not select a site merely because it is famous, visually attractive, easy to access or has an active volunteer group. It should first identify up to three candidate public sites and score them.

### 5.1 Site-selection scorecard

Score each criterion from 0 (not demonstrated) to 5 (strong evidence). Weighted score = criterion weight × score / 5.

| Criterion | Weight | What must be checked |
|---|---:|---|
| Legal authority, land custody and written access permission | 25% | Exact custodian, applicable corporation/agency, permitted survey area, written authorization |
| Ecological value and habitat diversity | 20% | Presence of suitable vegetation layers, habitat variety, green-blue connections and a meaningful survey question |
| Local partner and community readiness | 15% | Credible local group or institution, accessible participation, no assumption of community consent |
| Existing data and complementarity | 15% | Available tree census, site plans, biodiversity records and existing programmes; clear non-duplication |
| Safety and accessibility | 15% | Safe public access, field conditions, disability/accessibility considerations, emergency arrangements |
| Repeatability and learning value | 10% | Ability to revisit fixed survey points and transfer the protocol without overgeneralizing |

**Minimum selection threshold:** 70/100, with a score of at least 4/5 on legal authority and safety.

### 5.2 Automatic no-go conditions

Do not select or survey a site if:
- land custody or the authority to grant access is unclear;
- written permission is unavailable where required;
- the survey would disturb wildlife, damage vegetation or create safety risks;
- the site requires access to restricted or sensitive habitat;
- a credible local partner or responsible site contact cannot be identified;
- participation would expose residents, volunteers or knowledge holders to avoidable risk;
- the site is already subject to a similar survey and STF cannot demonstrate a useful, agreed complementary role.

### 5.3 Governance changes in Bengaluru

The Greater Bengaluru Governance Act, 2024 (Karnataka Act 36 of 2025) established the Greater Bengaluru Authority and provides for City Corporations and Ward Committees. The Act came into force in May 2025, and five City Corporations were notified in September 2025. The competent site authority must therefore be identified using the current corporation boundaries, asset records and agency responsibilities; historic BBMP references alone are insufficient.

References:
- India Code, Greater Bengaluru Governance Act, 2024, as amended: https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/21664/1/36_of_2025_%28e%29.pdf
- Government of Karnataka, Bengaluru West City Corporation — About GBA: https://bengaluruwest.karnataka.gov.in/about-gba

**Biodiversity Management Committee status is a specific verification item.** An August 2026 report stated that Bengaluru did not yet have a functioning city Biodiversity Management Committee following the governance transition, while national and state-level sources report BMCs and PBRs across Karnataka more generally. This status must be confirmed directly with the Greater Bengaluru Authority and Karnataka Biodiversity Board before any formal biodiversity-governance engagement. Do not assume that a functioning Bengaluru BMC exists, and do not assume that it does not without current written confirmation.

Reference for the reported status (not a substitute for official confirmation):  
https://bangaloremirror.indiatimes.com/bangalore/others/bengalurus-nature-shield-still-nowhere-in-sight/articleshow/133165237.cms

## 6. Proposed field method

The scientific lead must approve a written protocol before fieldwork. The following is a planning framework, not a substitute for expert method design.

### 6.1 Site mapping and habitat description

- Define the survey boundary and record its source, date and permission.
- Map broad habitat units, such as tree canopy, shrub layer, grass/herb layer, wet edge (if present), bare ground, built edge and paths.
- Record visible disturbance indicators using a simple, defined scale.
- Record survey time, weather, observer, effort and access limitations.
- Use existing official tree-inventory information where lawfully available; do not repeat a full tree census.

### 6.2 Plants

- Record vascular plants observable within fixed sample plots or transects designed by the botanist.
- Include trees, shrubs, climbers, herbs and grasses as the site and protocol require.
- Record diagnostic photographs where safe and lawful.
- Record native / non-native status only where supported by a named botanical reference and geographic context.
- Preserve uncertainty at genus or family level where species-level identification is not supported.
- Do not collect plant material, seeds, propagules or specimens.

### 6.3 Birds

- Use fixed observation points or transects, with a consistent duration and time window determined by the ornithologist.
- Record visual and auditory detections separately where possible.
- Record count method, distance/visibility limitations and repeat detections carefully.
- Do not approach nests, use playback, feed birds or disturb roosts.
- Do not publish sensitive nest or roost coordinates.

### 6.4 Butterflies

- Use a consistent, expert-selected transect or observation protocol.
- Record observer, time, weather, effort and identification confidence.
- Avoid capture or handling.
- Do not interpret butterfly counts as a complete measure of pollinator health or ecosystem function.

### 6.5 Repeat surveys and seasonality

Conduct at least three repeat rounds if the scientific lead considers the interval and season suitable. The final report must state the survey window and any seasonal limitations. Repeated visits over 12 weeks are not a substitute for a full annual or multi-season study.

### 6.6 Identification and data quality

Every record should contain, as applicable:
- observation identifier;
- date and local time;
- observer and protocol;
- site and survey unit;
- media reference;
- provisional identification;
- accepted identification and reviewer;
- identification confidence and evidence quality as separate fields;
- taxonomic source and version/date consulted;
- native-status source and geographic scope;
- uncertainty or unresolved status;
- data rights, consent and public-sharing status.

A photograph or volunteer suggestion is not, by itself, expert confirmation. The scientific lead should define a verification queue and sample-audit process.

## 7. Community participation and safeguards

The pilot should invite participation, not presume it. Public access to a site does not imply consent to photograph, interview or collect personal information from people using it.

- Explain the purpose, activities, data use, risks and withdrawal process before participation.
- Use Kannada and English materials where appropriate; test them with local users.
- Obtain explicit consent for identifiable photographs, interviews or personal stories.
- Do not collect personal data that is not needed.
- Establish safeguarding arrangements for minors; no child should be surveyed or photographed without appropriate institutional permission and guardian consent.
- Make participation accessible and avoid requiring specialist equipment.
- Provide fair reimbursement or honoraria for agreed work; do not build the budget around unpaid specialist labour.
- Provide a channel for complaints and corrections.
- Share findings with the site custodian and participants before public release.

**Traditional knowledge boundary:** This pilot does not collect or digitize traditional ecological knowledge, Adivasi knowledge, community plant-use knowledge or culturally restricted information. If a future project proposes this, it must be separately co-designed with the relevant rights holders, with prior agreement, appropriate governance, attribution, benefit-sharing and access controls.

## 8. Data governance, privacy and legal review

Before fieldwork, STF must complete a written applicability review with qualified Indian counsel and the relevant authorities.

The review must consider:
- the Biological Diversity Act, 2002, as amended in 2023;
- Biological Diversity Rules, 2024 and the 2025 amendment;
- current Karnataka Biodiversity Board guidance and the status/functions of the relevant local BMC, if any;
- applicable land, park, lake, forest, wildlife, photography and research permissions;
- privacy and personal-data obligations;
- data ownership, copyright, database rights and third-party source terms;
- whether any activity involves access to biological resources, associated traditional knowledge, commercial utilization, research or transfer that triggers specific legal requirements.

Official references:
- National Biodiversity Authority — Acts and Rules: https://nbaindia.nic.in/index.php/acts-and-rules/rules
- National Biodiversity Authority — FAQs and amendments: https://nbaindia.nic.in/about-us/faqs
- National Biodiversity Authority — Karnataka: https://nbaindia.nic.in/node/436?language_content_entity=en

This proposal is not a legal opinion. The fact that the pilot is non-invasive and photo-based does not automatically remove all permission or legal obligations.

### 8.1 Data handling rules

- Keep original observation files and metadata in a controlled STF workspace with role-based access.
- Maintain a data register showing source, rights holder, permitted uses, consent, attribution, review status and retention period.
- Do not assume that public visibility of a species observation grants STF a right to reuse its photograph or associated dataset.
- Do not upload third-party photographs or records to AI systems unless the applicable rights and permissions allow it.
- Generalize or withhold locations of rare, threatened, sensitive or vulnerable species when disclosure could create risk.
- Separate personal information from ecological records.
- Do not publish volunteer contact details, private property information or exact sensitive locations.
- Provide correction and takedown procedures.
- Retain unresolved identifications as unresolved; do not manufacture certainty.

## 9. Proposed 12-week implementation plan

The 12-week clock starts only after the initial authority, funding and safety gates are passed.

| Period | Work | Required output / gate |
|---|---|---|
| Weeks 1–2 | Confirm STF authority, named owner, available funds; identify up to three candidate sites; begin site-custody and BMC-status verification | Gate 0: authority and funding confirmed |
| Weeks 3–4 | Score sites; obtain written permissions; appoint scientific lead; approve protocol, safeguarding and data plan; complete legal review | Gate 1: one site and methods approved |
| Weeks 5–8 | Conduct desk review, mapping, volunteer orientation and repeat field-survey rounds | Field logs, raw observations, effort records and incident log |
| Weeks 9–10 | Expert identification review, data QA, participant feedback and gap analysis | Verified dataset, unresolved-record register and feedback summary |
| Weeks 11–12 | Independent technical review, final report, budget reconciliation and continuation assessment | Gate 2: go / redesign / pause / stop recommendation |

If permissions or a suitable site are not secured by the end of Week 4, the project should pause rather than compress fieldwork or bypass safeguards.

## 10. Deliverables

1. Site-selection scorecard and documented site decision.
2. Written permissions and responsibility map.
3. Approved field protocol and risk assessment.
4. Site boundary and habitat map, with sensitive information controlled.
5. Plant, bird and butterfly observation register with provenance and verification status.
6. Volunteer training materials and participation summary.
7. Data-quality report, including unresolved and excluded records.
8. Short baseline report with limitations and recommendations.
9. Independent technical review note.
10. Final expenditure report and continuation decision.

The public-facing report should explain what was observed, what was not observed, survey effort, limitations, identification uncertainty and data rights. It must not describe the pilot as a restoration success or biodiversity increase.

## 11. Proposed indicators

| Indicator | Measurement | Interpretation limit |
|---|---|---|
| Survey coverage | Number of completed protocol-compliant visits / planned visits | Measures delivery, not ecological improvement |
| Plant records | Number of records by taxonomic confidence level | Not necessarily total site plant richness |
| Bird records | Species and detections recorded under defined effort | Detectability varies; not a population census |
| Butterfly records | Species and detections under defined protocol | Not a complete pollinator assessment |
| Verification quality | Share of records reviewed; unresolved rate; correction rate | A high verification rate does not prove complete coverage |
| Habitat documentation | Mapped habitat units and disturbance observations | Not a full habitat-condition assessment |
| Volunteer data quality | Agreement between volunteer records and expert audit sample | Measures protocol performance |
| Participation | Number and diversity of participants, accessibility feedback | Participation is not conservation impact |
| Governance readiness | Permissions, partner roles, data terms and maintenance owner documented | A checklist does not replace legitimate consent |
| Continuation readiness | Scientific, legal, financial and partner review passed | Does not automatically authorize expansion |

Do not set a target for the number of species discovered before the site and protocol are known. Avoid rewarding participants for high counts, which can encourage duplicate or low-quality records.

## 12. Roles and responsibilities

| Role | Minimum responsibility |
|---|---|
| STF accountable owner | Scope, funding, approvals, risk ownership and public claims |
| Scientific lead (qualified ecologist/botanist) | Survey design, field supervision, identification standards and scientific sign-off |
| Ornithology / butterfly specialist | Review protocols and difficult records in their taxonomic groups |
| Field coordinator | Scheduling, safety briefings, consent and field records |
| Data steward | Rights, metadata, access control, retention, sensitive locations and correction requests |
| Local partner / site contact | Local coordination and site-specific guidance within their authority |
| Independent reviewer | Review method, sample records, uncertainty and conclusions |
| Finance/operations lead | Contracts, payments, receipts, budget tracking and close-out |

The person delivering the field survey should not be the sole person approving the final scientific conclusions.

No partner, institution or public authority is assumed to have agreed to participate. Names should be added only after direct engagement and written confirmation.

## 14. Risks and mitigations

| Risk | Mitigation / response |
|---|---|
| Wrong or unclear site authority | Verify custodian and current GBA/City Corporation jurisdiction; obtain written permission |
| Bengaluru BMC status unclear | Confirm directly with GBA and Karnataka Biodiversity Board before relying on BMC/PBR structures |
| Duplication of existing programmes | Map Kere Mitra, LakeRevive, existing tree census and local research; agree a distinct role |
| Misidentification | Expert review, evidence grades, unresolved category and correction process |
| Seasonal bias | Disclose survey window; no annual trend claims |
| Volunteer disturbance to wildlife | Training, no handling/feeding/playback/nest approach; supervised access |
| Sensitive species exposure | Generalize or withhold exact coordinates |
| Personal-data misuse | Minimize personal data, consent and access controls |
| Unauthorised use of third-party images/data | Rights register and permission check before reuse |
| Community tokenism | Participation is voluntary; local priorities and concerns influence design; no TK collection in this pilot |
| Budget overrun | ₹4 lakh cap and prior approval for all commitments |
| Pilot becomes a campaign before evidence exists | No public impact claims until independent review |
| No continuity after 12 weeks | Name a potential data custodian and maintenance owner before recommending expansion |

## 15. Decision gates

### Gate 0 — Permission to scope

Required before any external commitment:
- STF authorized owner and governing approval identified;
- cash availability and ₹4 lakh maximum budget confirmed;
- legal status of STF and contracting capacity understood;
- initial legal and safeguarding questions assigned;
- no public claim that a pilot has been launched.

### Gate 1 — Permission to conduct fieldwork

Required before any survey:
- selected site scores at least 70/100 and passes mandatory legal/safety thresholds;
- exact land/site custodian confirmed;
- written access permission obtained;
- scientific lead and protocols appointed and approved;
- insurance/safety and safeguarding arrangements confirmed;
- data rights, privacy, sensitive-location and retention plan approved;
- relevant GBA/City Corporation, Karnataka Biodiversity Board and BMC status checked as applicable.

### Gate 2 — Continuation decision

At Week 12, the authorized STF decision-maker chooses one:
- **Go:** only if scientific quality, local legitimacy, legal compliance, partner capacity, funding and a longer monitoring rationale are all credible.
- **Redesign:** if the question or protocol is useful but the site, partner or method needs adjustment.
- **Pause:** if seasonal coverage, authority, budget or partner readiness is incomplete.
- **Stop:** if the project duplicates existing work, lacks legitimacy, cannot protect data or cannot produce a useful and responsible outcome.

A high score cannot compensate for absent consent, unlawful activity, missing required permissions, serious ecological risk or inability to protect sensitive information.

## 16. What success looks like

The pilot is successful if STF:
- selects a site transparently and with permission;
- produces a small, reliable and traceable baseline;
- reports uncertainty and limitations honestly;
- demonstrates that citizen observations can be useful when properly designed and checked;
- complements rather than duplicates existing Bengaluru work;
- builds a respectful local relationship without extracting knowledge;
- finishes within the approved budget;
- makes a defensible decision to continue, redesign, pause or stop.

A species count, social-media reach, event attendance or number of photographs is not, by itself, a conservation outcome.

## 17. Recommendation and proposed decision

**Recommendation:** Proceed to Gate 0 only. Do not yet announce the pilot, choose a lake, recruit volunteers, approach communities for knowledge, enter a partnership, or begin field surveys.

The next practical step is a short internal readiness exercise:
1. Confirm STF's legal and financial ability to contract and hold funds.
2. Appoint the accountable owner.
3. Confirm whether up to ₹4 lakh is genuinely available without compromising core Botanica Indica scientific work.
4. Commission a desk-based shortlist of up to three candidate public sites.
5. Confirm current site authority and Bengaluru BMC status.
6. Invite a qualified urban ecologist/botanist to review the proposed question and budget.

If any of these conditions cannot be met, defer the pilot. The absence of a pilot is preferable to an underfunded or scientifically weak conservation project.

**Decision status:** Proposed for review. No programme, expenditure, partnership, field activity, conservation intervention or public impact claim is approved by this document.
