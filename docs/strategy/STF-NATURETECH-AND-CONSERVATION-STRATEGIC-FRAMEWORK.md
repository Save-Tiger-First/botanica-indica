# Save Tiger First — NatureTech and Conservation Strategic Framework

**Status:** Proposed strategic framework for Board, scientific and community review  
**Version:** 0.1  
**Date:** 16 November 2026  
**Related strategy:** [STF NatureTech, Biodiversity Intelligence and Conservation Impact](STF-NATURETECH-BIODIVERSITY-INTELLIGENCE-AND-CONSERVATION-IMPACT.md)  
**Decision authority:** Save Tiger First's authorized governing body  
**Important:** This document is a proposal. It is not an approved organizational policy, conservation plan, scientific finding, legal opinion, community agreement or commitment by any external partner.

---

## 1. Executive proposition

Save Tiger First (STF) should develop as an evidence-led, rights-respecting conservation and biodiversity-intelligence initiative that uses technology where it demonstrably helps people understand, monitor and protect biodiversity.

STF should not define success by the number of applications, features, observations, datasets, events or awareness campaigns it produces. It should define success through a credible chain of results:

1. Better-quality biodiversity knowledge and evidence.
2. Better-informed people, institutions and decisions.
3. Practical actions that address identified pressures.
4. Measurable improvements in habitat condition, ecological processes or species outcomes where STF and its partners can reasonably contribute and assess them.

STF should begin with a disciplined first domain—Botanica Indica—while developing partnerships and a limited number of carefully selected conservation-learning pilots. New domains and programmes should be added only when a demonstrated need, qualified leadership, governance, funding and measurable outcomes justify them.

## 2. Vision, mission and proposed strategic ambition

### 2.1 Vision

A world in which people and institutions understand biodiversity, respect the communities and knowledge systems connected to it, and make better decisions that support nature.

### 2.2 Mission

To develop and support evidence-based biodiversity intelligence, accessible learning and collaborative conservation action, using appropriate technology and accountable partnerships.

### 2.3 Strategic ambition

STF aims to connect scientific knowledge, local observation, community stewardship, education and practical decision-making—without claiming that a digital platform alone can deliver conservation outcomes.

### 2.4 What STF is not

STF is not proposed as:
- A substitute for statutory conservation agencies, universities, museums, botanical gardens, local institutions or community governance.
- A universal authority on species identification or ecological condition.
- A repository entitled to collect or publish all biodiversity or traditional knowledge.
- A conservation-impact certifier without suitable methods and independent evidence.
- A technology-first venture whose growth is measured primarily by downloads, features or data volume.

## 3. Six complementary strategic dimensions

The following dimensions synthesize the six talks and associated research considered in the companion strategy. The talks are sources of questions and perspectives, not substitutes for peer-reviewed evidence or community authorization.

### 3.1 NatureTech: technology in service of nature

Use digital tools, AI, geospatial systems, sensors and data infrastructure only where they solve a defined ecological, scientific, educational or operational problem.

Principles:
- Start with the ecological or user need, not the technology.
- Make uncertainty, provenance and limitations visible.
- Provide a valid “unknown / insufficient evidence” outcome.
- Keep humans accountable for high-consequence decisions.
- Assess the environmental and social costs of technology itself.

### 3.2 Human–nature connectedness and learning

Help people develop meaningful understanding of local nature and their relationship with it.

Possible approaches:
- Place-based biodiversity learning.
- Guided observation and interpretation.
- School and community programmes.
- Nature journaling and accessible citizen science.
- Reflection and learning activities designed with inclusion and safety in mind.

Measure participation and learning separately from behaviour change and ecological impact. Do not make unqualified therapeutic or health claims.

### 3.3 Ecological relationships and interdependence

Represent biodiversity as interacting organisms, populations, habitats and ecological processes—not simply isolated species records.

Potential subject areas include plant–pollinator relationships, plant–fungus associations, herbivory, seed dispersal, soil processes and food-web interactions. Record the evidence type, geographic and temporal scope, uncertainty and source. Avoid anthropomorphic or overstated claims.

### 3.4 Hidden and microbial biodiversity

Recognize that biodiversity includes microorganisms, soil communities and ecosystem processes that are not visible in ordinary field observations.

Potential future work:
- Partner-led soil and microbial biodiversity studies.
- Land-use change and habitat-quality research.
- Carefully governed environmental genomics.
- Research translation and education.

Do not infer microbial composition, function, medical value or ecosystem health from generic global statistics. Each claim needs context-specific sampling and appropriate laboratory and analytical methods.

### 3.5 Indigenous and local stewardship, TEK and data governance

Respect the rights, authority, priorities and knowledge governance of Indigenous Peoples and local communities.

Minimum principles:
- No assumption that knowledge is free to collect because it is visible, spoken publicly or recorded online.
- Identify the appropriate knowledge holders and decision-making authorities with the community.
- Obtain consent and permissions for each intended purpose, not a blanket permission.
- Agree on access, attribution, confidentiality, benefit-sharing, retention, withdrawal and downstream use.
- Do not expose sacred, restricted, sensitive or location-sensitive knowledge by default.
- Do not use community knowledge, genetic resources or associated data for AI training or commercial purposes without explicit, valid authorization and applicable legal compliance.
- Apply CARE (Collective Benefit, Authority to Control, Responsibility, Ethics) alongside relevant FAIR data practices; FAIR does not override rights or consent.

### 3.6 Urban biodiversity and ecological connectivity

Treat cities and towns as parts of wider ecological systems. Urban green and blue spaces, buildings, wetlands, lakes, rivers, trees, gardens and infrastructure can affect habitat availability and connectivity.

Potential future work:
- Local urban biodiversity baselines.
- Biodiversity-sensitive planning and building guidance.
- Protection of existing habitat before installing artificial structures.
- Species-specific interventions designed with qualified experts.
- Seasonal monitoring and maintenance.
- Participation by residents, schools, building owners, planners and local authorities.

The Beijing Swift is an illustrative case of migratory connectivity and urban nesting—not a template to copy for Indian species.

## 4. Strategic outcomes and theory of change

STF should use a contribution-based theory of change. It should not claim sole attribution for ecological changes influenced by many actors and drivers.

| Level | Meaning | Examples of evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs | Resources invested | Staff time, funding, partner capacity, equipment |
| Activities | Work performed | Research, platform development, training, surveys |
| Outputs | Direct products | Validated profiles, datasets, learning sessions, survey reports |
| Knowledge outcomes | Better understanding | Assessment results, improved identification literacy, documented uncertainty |
| Decision and behaviour outcomes | Changes in practice | Habitat retained, data corrections, building plans revised, responsible reporting |
| Ecological outcomes | Changes in nature | Occupancy, recruitment, habitat condition, native species trends, ecological connectivity |
| Long-term impact | Durable contribution to biodiversity | Supported by longitudinal and independent evidence, with external drivers acknowledged |

Every programme should specify which levels it can realistically measure. An activity or output must never be reported as proof of ecological impact.

## 5. Strategic portfolio and sequencing

### Phase 0 — Establish organizational foundations

Before expanding programme activity:
- Approve mission, scope and governance through STF's authorized process.
- Clarify legal status, responsibilities, financial controls and risk ownership.
- Establish scientific, data, privacy, safeguarding and rights review procedures.
- Define partner selection and conflict-of-interest rules.
- Establish a realistic funding and operating model.

### Phase 1 — Prove the first domain: Botanica Indica

Priorities:
- Validate the scientific data model with independent specialists.
- Build a traceable, rights-aware botanical information foundation.
- Establish transparent identification confidence and evidence quality.
- Validate a narrow set of Indian geographic and taxonomic use cases.
- Test user value and scientific performance with representative users.
- Establish data correction, expert review and source-update procedures.

Do not move to physical production design or claim scientific validation before the documented review gates are met.

### Phase 2 — Run a small number of partnership pilots

Select at most one or two pilots where:
- A real conservation or learning need is documented.
- A qualified local partner is committed.
- Community rights and statutory requirements are understood.
- Baseline and outcome measures are feasible.
- Funding, safety, permissions and maintenance responsibilities exist.
- The pilot can be evaluated and stopped if it does not work.

Potential pilot themes—not approved programmes—include urban biodiversity, school-based nature learning, local plant knowledge with community governance, and partner-led habitat monitoring.

### Phase 3 — Evaluate before scaling

Scale only after:
- Independent review of scientific methods and results.
- Evidence that intended users or partners benefit.
- Evidence of ecological or decision outcomes where claimed.
- Governance and operating costs are sustainable.
- Risks and unintended effects are understood.
- Local partners agree that expansion is appropriate.

### Phase 4 — Consider additional STF domains

A new domain should require a distinct domain problem, accountable subject-matter leadership, a viable data and rights model, partner demand, a credible product case and a funded operating plan. Shared infrastructure is a means of reducing duplication, not a reason to create additional apps.

## 6. Programme selection scorecard

Score each proposed programme from 0 to 5 for each criterion. Record evidence and reviewer comments; do not treat the total as an automatic approval.

| Criterion | Key question |
|---|---|
| Biodiversity relevance | Is there a defined and material biodiversity problem or opportunity? |
| Evidence quality | Is the proposed intervention supported by appropriate evidence? |
| Local legitimacy | Are relevant local institutions and rights holders involved appropriately? |
| Additionality | Does STF add value beyond existing initiatives? |
| Measurability | Can outputs, outcomes and limitations be measured credibly? |
| Feasibility | Are skills, partners, permissions, time and resources available? |
| Rights and ethics | Are consent, data rights, privacy and benefit-sharing addressed? |
| Durability | Who maintains the intervention and supports it after the pilot? |
| Risk | Are ecological, social, legal, reputational and technical risks manageable? |
| Learning value | Will the pilot produce transferable learning without overgeneralization? |

**No-go rule:** A high score cannot compensate for absent consent, unlawful activity, unmanageable ecological risk, lack of required permissions or inability to protect sensitive data.

## 7. Measurement framework

Use a small, programme-specific indicator set. Define baseline, method, frequency, responsible party, data owner, uncertainty and reporting limitations for every indicator.

### 7.1 Platform and scientific quality
- Taxonomic name and concept provenance completeness.
- Expert-reviewed record rate, with reviewer qualifications.
- Identification performance by taxonomic group, geography, image quality and user cohort.
- Calibration and abstention performance, not just top-1 accuracy.
- Error correction and appeal resolution time.
- Source currency and rights metadata completeness.

### 7.2 Learning and participation
- Participation and retention, disaggregated only where lawful and appropriate.
- Pre/post knowledge assessment.
- Ability to distinguish evidence from inference and uncertainty.
- Accessibility and inclusion measures.
- Participant-reported relevance, with appropriate safeguards.

### 7.3 Decision and behaviour outcomes
- Documented decisions informed by STF evidence.
- Confirmed habitat or nest-site retention actions.
- Adopted biodiversity-sensitive design or management changes.
- Partner commitments with named responsibility and maintenance.
- Community-defined benefits and satisfaction, where communities choose to evaluate them.

### 7.4 Ecological outcomes
Select measures suitable to the intervention, such as:
- Species occupancy or breeding outcomes.
- Habitat extent, condition and connectivity.
- Native species composition and regeneration.
- Survey-detected trends using consistent methods.
- Soil or water indicators with appropriate sampling design.
- Threat reduction or habitat-pressure indicators.

Do not infer population recovery from sightings or app records alone. Account for detectability, sampling effort, seasonality, observer bias, spatial coverage and external drivers.

## 8. Governance and accountability

Proposed roles, subject to STF's legal and organizational structure:

- **Governing body:** Approves mission, risk appetite, budgets, strategy and major commitments.
- **Executive/programme lead:** Accountable for delivery, partner management and resources.
- **Scientific advisory function:** Reviews methods, claims, taxonomy, ecological interpretation and publication standards.
- **Data and technology lead:** Owns architecture, security, privacy, provenance, access and operational reliability.
- **Community and rights lead:** Ensures engagement, consent, authority, grievance and benefit-sharing processes are designed with relevant communities.
- **Independent evaluators:** Review selected outcomes and claims where independence is necessary.

Advisory roles must not be described as community endorsement or formal scientific approval unless explicitly authorized and documented.

## 9. Data, AI and technology safeguards

1. Separate scientific assertions, source records, observations, expert determinations and model outputs.
2. Preserve source provenance, dates, versions, licenses and transformations.
3. Keep confidence, evidence quality and taxonomic certainty distinct.
4. Record AI model version, training-data rights, evaluation scope and known limitations.
5. Provide abstention and escalation pathways when evidence is insufficient.
6. Apply privacy-by-design and generalize or withhold sensitive species and community locations where appropriate.
7. Do not infer permission to redistribute or train AI from permission to view or upload data.
8. Keep public display, research access, commercial use and model training as separate rights decisions.
9. Use open standards where useful, but do not confuse interoperability with unrestricted reuse.
10. Avoid microservices and infrastructure complexity before operational needs justify them.

## 10. India implementation context

The framework should align with India's applicable biodiversity governance, laws and institutional arrangements, while avoiding the assumption that a single permission or process applies to every activity.

Relevant institutions and instruments may include:
- National Biodiversity Authority and State Biodiversity Boards.
- Biodiversity Management Committees and People's Biodiversity Registers.
- Local bodies, Gram Sabhas and community institutions, where relevant.
- Forest Rights Act processes and rights, where relevant.
- Biological Diversity Act and applicable rules, regulations, exemptions and access-and-benefit-sharing requirements.
- State and local biodiversity strategies, land-use planning and urban development processes.
- Universities, botanical gardens, museums, herbaria, research institutes and conservation organizations.

India's updated National Biodiversity Strategy and Action Plan 2024–2030 includes urban biodiversity indicators, including the number of biodiversity parks, botanical/public gardens, water bodies and nature-learning centres, and the number of cities with a City Biodiversity Index. These provide relevant reference points for programme alignment, not automatic endorsement or proof of impact.

For urban work, the Convention on Biological Diversity's Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework Target 12 is particularly relevant: it calls for improvements in the area, quality, connectivity, access and benefits of urban green and blue spaces, alongside biodiversity-inclusive planning and native biodiversity. Target 12 should be treated as a strategic alignment reference, not as a claim that STF is implementing or fulfilling the target.

Obtain qualified Indian legal advice for specific projects, particularly where biological resources, associated traditional knowledge, access, research, commercial use, protected areas, forests, wildlife, personal data or community rights may be involved.

## 11. Partnership principles

Prioritize reciprocal, clearly scoped partnerships with:
- Universities and research institutions.
- Botanical, zoological and natural history institutions.
- Conservation organizations and field ecologists.
- Indigenous Peoples and local communities through their own legitimate authorities and chosen processes.
- Schools, educators and nature-learning organizations.
- Urban planners, architects, landscape professionals and local governments.
- Technology and data partners with transparent rights and security practices.

Every partnership should specify purpose, authority, roles, costs, data ownership and access, publication, attribution, safeguarding, benefit-sharing, liability, duration, exit and dispute resolution.

## 12. Risks and safeguards

| Risk | Required safeguard |
|---|---|
| Technology-first expansion | Problem and evidence gate before product approval |
| Misidentification or false certainty | Validation, uncertainty, abstention and expert escalation |
| Sensitive location disclosure | Tiered access, spatial generalization and threat assessment |
| Knowledge extraction | Community authority, consent, benefit-sharing and grievance mechanism |
| Rights infringement | Asset-level rights registry and legal review |
| Tokenistic participation | Community-defined roles, resources and decision authority |
| Greenwashing / impact inflation | Results chain, transparent limitations and independent evaluation |
| Unsustainable pilots | Named maintenance owner and funded lifecycle |
| Poor ecological intervention | Baseline, species-specific design and monitoring |
| Mission drift | Portfolio review and governing-body approval |

## 13. Decision gates

A proposal progresses only when the relevant gate is satisfied.

- **Gate A — Strategic fit:** Problem, beneficiaries, scope and STF additionality documented.
- **Gate B — Scientific validity:** Methods, sources, uncertainty and expert review plan approved.
- **Gate C — Rights and legitimacy:** Consent, legal applicability, data rights and community governance addressed.
- **Gate D — Delivery readiness:** Partners, budget, staffing, permissions, safety and maintenance confirmed.
- **Gate E — Evaluation readiness:** Baseline, indicators, methods and reporting responsibilities established.
- **Gate F — Scale decision:** Results reviewed, limitations published, and governing body approves continuation or scale.

A failed or incomplete gate means pause, redesign or stop—not automatic progression.

## 14. First 12-month priorities after formal approval

The sequence below is indicative and must be adjusted to actual capacity and funding.

1. Establish governance, scientific advisory arrangements and rights review process.
2. Complete independent validation of Botanica Indica's logical scientific data model.
3. Define and test a narrow Botanica Indica MVP with Indian botanical use cases.
4. Establish source and media rights registry and sensitive-location safeguards.
5. Conduct user research and partner discovery with documented consent and safeguards.
6. Select one potential pilot only after scorecard and no-go review.
7. Establish baseline and evaluation protocol before pilot activity.
8. Publish a transparent annual progress report distinguishing activities, outputs, outcomes and ecological evidence.

Do not commit to all eight items without a resourced operating plan. Prioritize quality over the number of workstreams.

## 15. Strategic decisions requested

The authorized STF governing body is invited to decide whether to:

1. Endorse the proposed vision, mission and six strategic dimensions for further development.
2. Confirm Botanica Indica as the first domain priority, subject to scientific validation.
3. Adopt the principle that no new domain or conservation pilot proceeds without the decision gates.
4. Commission qualified legal, scientific and community-governance review.
5. Authorize development of a realistic operating plan and budget.
6. Define which commitments STF is prepared to make publicly and which remain exploratory.

No decision should be inferred from circulation or discussion of this draft.
